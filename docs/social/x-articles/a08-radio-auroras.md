# Listening for the Auroras of Other Worlds

**Planets with magnetic fields have auroras — and auroras can give off radio waves. Jupiter does it. Earth does it. Some brown dwarfs do it. Could radio surveys help find worlds we can't see?**

> **Suggested header image:** Illustration of a gas-giant planet with glowing aurora rings at its poles, and faint radio waves spreading outward toward a radio telescope dish.
> **Alt text:** "Illustration: a giant planet with glowing auroras at both poles sends radio waves toward a radio telescope dish. Auroras on magnetic planets can produce strong radio signals."

**Evidence level:** 🟢 **Level 1 — Confirmed** that Jupiter, Earth and some brown dwarfs give off auroral radio waves. 🔵 **Level 2 — Strong evidence** for radio signals from some red dwarf stars that may come from star–planet interaction. 🟡 **Level 3 — Testable idea** that low-frequency radio archives contain signals from still-unknown brown dwarfs or exoplanets.

---

Have you ever seen pictures of the northern lights — green and purple ribbons dancing in the sky? Those are **auroras**. They happen when fast particles from space are steered by Earth's magnetic field and crash into the air near the poles.

Here is something surprising: auroras don't just glow. They can also "sing" in radio waves. You can't hear them with your ears, but radio antennas can pick them up.

## Jupiter's radio voice

In 1955, two radio astronomers, Bernard Burke and Kenneth Franklin, were surprised to find strong radio bursts coming from Jupiter. Jupiter has a huge magnetic field and powerful auroras. Its radio signals are so strong that people can listen to them on Earth with simple antennas. NASA's **Radio JOVE** education project helps students build kits to do just that.

## Earth sings too

Spacecraft have found that Earth's auroras give off intense radio waves, called **auroral kilometric radiation**. We can't hear them from the ground because Earth's upper atmosphere (the **ionosphere**) blocks those low frequencies. From space, though, Earth is a bright radio source.

## Brown dwarfs with auroras

In 2015, a team led by Gregg Hallinan reported that a brown dwarf — a "failed star" — has auroras much more powerful than Jupiter's. Since then, radio telescopes have detected many radio-loud brown dwarfs. In 2020, a team using the **LOFAR** radio telescope in Europe even reported finding a cold brown dwarf **by its radio signal first**, then confirming it with other telescopes.

## A possible planet's radio "fingerprint"

Also in 2020, Harish Vedantham and colleagues reported unusual radio waves from a quiet red dwarf star called **GJ 1151**. The signal looked like the kind of radio emission that happens when a planet's motion disturbs a star's magnetic field, a bit like how Jupiter's moon Io triggers radio bursts from Jupiter. They suggested an Earth-sized planet in a very short orbit could explain it. Follow-up measurements did not find a large planet close to the star, but a small one is still possible. Other studies have since found many radio-loud red dwarfs.

## What is still open

> Do public low-frequency radio surveys already contain auroral signals from brown dwarfs or exoplanets that no one has identified yet?

Radio surveys like LOFAR's map large areas of the sky. Some faint, polarized radio sources may be nearby cool objects that are hard to see in other light.

## How to test it

1. Start with public radio survey catalogs.
2. Look for sources with strong **circular polarization** — auroral radio waves often twist in one direction.
3. Check if the source is near a known nearby star or brown dwarf, or if it moves over time.
4. Compare with infrared catalogs (like WISE) for a faint, cold counterpart.
5. Look for repeating bursts that might match a rotation period or an orbit.

## How we could be wrong

- **Radio interference** from human technology is everywhere. It must be ruled out carefully.
- Many polarized radio sources are distant galaxies or pulsars, not nearby objects.
- Flares on ordinary stars can produce radio bursts that look similar.
- A planet's own radio signal is usually at very low frequencies, which Earth's ionosphere blocks. Many planet signals may be impossible to hear from the ground.

## How you can help

- **Kids and families:** Listen to recordings of Jupiter's radio sounds (NASA has shared some). Then tune an AM radio between stations and listen to the hiss. Real radio astronomy means separating signals from noise.
- **Students:** Learn about Radio JOVE and how simple antennas can pick up Jupiter.
- **Data fans:** Explore public radio survey catalogs and compare them with infrared maps. Write down how you would rule out interference.
