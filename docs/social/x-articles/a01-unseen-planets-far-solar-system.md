# Can We Find a Planet by Its Pull Before We See It?

**It worked once before, in 1846. Today, spacecraft tracking, pulsar timing and the new Rubin Observatory are testing whether another unseen world lies far beyond Neptune.**

> **Suggested header image:** Diagram of the outer Solar System showing the orbits of Neptune and several distant icy objects, with a dashed question-mark orbit far outside them.
> **Alt text:** "Diagram: Neptune's orbit and the stretched orbits of distant icy objects. A dashed orbit with a question mark shows where an undiscovered planet could be, if it exists."

**Evidence level:** 🟡 **Level 3 — Testable idea.** Finding planets by their pull is 🟢 Level 1 — Confirmed (it is how Neptune was found). Whether another large planet exists far beyond Neptune is still an open question.

---

Imagine you are standing on a big trampoline in a dark room. Somewhere on it is a heavy bowling ball. You can't see it. But you can feel it. The trampoline dips toward it. Marbles you roll curve toward it.

You don't need a flashlight to find that ball. You can find it by watching how other things move.

Astronomers can do the same thing in space.

## The planet found with math

In the 1800s, astronomers noticed that the planet Uranus was not moving exactly as predicted. Something seemed to be tugging on it. The French mathematician Urbain Le Verrier calculated where an unknown planet would have to be to cause that tug. In September 1846, astronomer Johann Galle pointed a telescope at that spot and found Neptune.

That is 🟢 **Level 1 — Confirmed.** Gravity can reveal a planet before anyone sees it.

## Is there another one?

Far beyond Neptune, there are many small icy worlds. Some of them have very long, stretched orbits. In 2016, astronomers Konstantin Batygin and Michael Brown pointed out that several of these orbits seem to line up in a similar direction. They suggested a large, unseen planet — often called "Planet Nine" — might be shaping them.

Other scientists have questioned this. Some studies argue that the lining-up could come from *where and when* telescopes happened to look, not from a real planet. This debate is healthy. It is how science sorts out real signals from accidents.

So the idea is 🟡 **Level 3 — Testable idea.** Not proven. Not ruled out.

## Clocks in space that feel the pull

A large hidden planet would tug, very slightly, on everything in the Solar System — including spacecraft and even Earth itself.

- **Spacecraft tracking.** Radio signals sent to spacecraft like Cassini, which orbited Saturn, let scientists measure distances with great precision. Researchers have used this kind of data to rule out some places where a large planet could be.
- **Pulsar timing.** Pulsars are dead stars that spin and send out radio pulses like the ticks of a very precise clock. To use them, astronomers must know exactly where Earth is compared with the center of mass of the Solar System. An unknown planet would shift that center a little. Pulsar timing teams have used this to set limits on how heavy an unknown planet could be.

These methods are excellent at saying **where a planet is not**. Each new limit makes the search area smaller.

## A brand-new telescope joins the hunt

The Vera C. Rubin Observatory in Chile is now mapping the southern sky again and again. In October 2026, the Rubin team reported a large, distant icy object called **2026 ED138**. Early estimates put its size somewhere between about 600 and 1,500 kilometers, and it is roughly 46 times farther from the Sun than Earth is. It is a dwarf-planet candidate — not Planet Nine — but it shows how powerful this new survey is.

If a distant giant planet exists in the part of the sky Rubin can see, it has a real chance of finding it.

## What is still open

Our disclosure asks a careful question:

> Can we combine the limits from spacecraft tracking, pulsar timing and sky surveys to make a clear, public "map of where a distant planet could still be"?

That map would help telescope teams decide where to look. It would also make the debate easier for everyone to follow.

## How we could be wrong

- The lining-up of distant orbits might be a survey effect. If larger, fairer surveys (like Rubin's) show no clustering, the main reason to expect a planet goes away.
- The planet, if it exists, might be smaller or farther than models suggest. Then its pull would be too weak to detect with today's data.
- Small errors in how we model the asteroid belt or other known bodies can look like tiny tugs. They must be ruled out first.

## How you can help

- **Kids and families:** Try the trampoline activity. Put a heavy ball under a stretched bedsheet and roll marbles across. Can a friend guess where the ball is just by watching the marbles?
- **Students:** Read about how Neptune was found. Then read the 2016 Planet Nine paper's summary and one paper that disagrees. What evidence would change your mind?
- **Data fans:** Follow Rubin Observatory's public data releases. New distant objects help test the clustering idea.
