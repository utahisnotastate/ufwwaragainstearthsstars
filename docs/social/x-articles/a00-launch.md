# Where Tomorrow's Space Discoveries Might Already Be Hiding

**Introducing Future Disclosure: Astronomy & Alien Planets — an open, honest, kid-friendly guide to 15 places where new discoveries may be waiting in public science data.**

---

Imagine a giant library where new books arrive every night. The books are full of pictures of the sky. There are so many books that no one can read them all. Some pages have never been looked at closely.

That library is real. It is the collection of public data from telescopes and spacecraft. And some of tomorrow's discoveries are probably already written on pages no one has studied yet.

That is what this project is about.

## What we are launching

**Future Disclosure: Astronomy & Alien Planets** is a free, open-source project. It has:

- **15 "disclosures."** Each one is a short, honest report about a place where a discovery *might* be hiding in public data.
- **Free code** (a Python package called `fdastro`) that anyone can use to explore real astronomy problems.
- **Lessons and activities** for kids, families, teachers and tutors.
- **Guides** for physicists, astrophysicists, students, amateur astronomers, space agency professionals and SETI researchers.

Everything is written in two layers. The top of each page is simple enough for a curious 10-year-old. The lower part has the equations, data sources and references that experts need.

## What "disclosure" means here

We know the word "disclosure" can sound like secret files. That is **not** what we mean.

A disclosure here is a short report that says three things:

1. **What might be hiding** in public data.
2. **How sure we are.**
3. **How anyone could check it** — and how the idea could turn out to be wrong.

Nothing is secret. The data is public. The methods are published. Scientists and archive teams worked hard to collect and share all of it. There is simply more data than anyone has had time to study.

## How sure are we? Evidence levels

Every claim gets a label:

- 🟢 **Level 1 — Confirmed:** seen and checked more than once.
- 🔵 **Level 2 — Strong evidence:** good published results, still discussed by experts.
- 🟡 **Level 3 — Testable idea:** follows normal physics, not shown yet, and you can help test it.
- 🟠 **Level 4 — Long shot:** physics allows it, but today's tools may be too weak.

Most of our 15 ideas are Level 3. That means they are real questions with real tests — not answers yet.

## A taste of what's coming

Here are a few of the topics we will share, one per week starting **12 January 2027**:

- **Interstellar visitors.** Three objects from other star systems have been found passing through ours: 1I/ʻOumuamua (2017), 2I/Borisov (2019) and 3I/ATLAS (2025). Could more be sitting unrecognized in lists of unlinked asteroid sightings?
- **Planets whose transits fade away.** In 2026, astronomers reported that the planet TOI-2535 b stopped passing in front of its star, probably because a hidden neighbor is tilting its orbit. How many more are out there?
- **Cold, dark neighbors.** Infrared sky maps have revealed cold "failed stars" near the Sun. Volunteers have helped find many of them. Are there more?
- **Moons around other planets.** No moon around a planet outside our Solar System has been confirmed yet. A clever timing test might help.
- **Visitors that stayed.** Some objects orbit the Sun backwards. A few scientists think one of them might have come from another star long ago. Others disagree. How could we tell?

## What we are *not* doing

- We are **not** claiming that anyone hid anything.
- We are **not** claiming evidence of alien life or alien technology. "Alien planets" here means planets around other stars.
- We are **not** giving health or wellness advice.
- We are **not** announcing discoveries. If anyone using our tools finds something interesting, it goes through careful checks and is reported to the right scientific organization — like the Minor Planet Center — before anything is shared publicly.

## Why do this?

Because looking at real data is the best way to learn real science. A child who measures how far a "star" jumps when they blink one eye has learned parallax. A teenager who fits an orbit to real sky positions has done the same math professionals use. And sometimes, people outside big observatories make real discoveries. Volunteers in citizen-science projects have helped find brown dwarfs, planets and more.

## How you can help

- **Read along.** One disclosure each Tuesday, starting 12 January 2027.
- **Try an activity** with your kids or class. Most need only string, a flashlight or a ball.
- **Run the code** if you like Python. Start with the installation tutorial in the repository.
- **Tell us when we are wrong.** Open a correction request in the repository. We fix mistakes in public and thank the people who find them.
- **Share kindly.** If you share our posts, please keep the evidence level with them.

*Kids: please ask a parent, guardian or teacher before posting or commenting online.*
