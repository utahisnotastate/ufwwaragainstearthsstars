# Planets With No Star, Caught in a Flash of Light

**Some planets drift through the galaxy alone. We can't see them directly — but when one passes in front of a distant star, its gravity briefly magnifies that star's light. The smaller the planet, the shorter the flash.**

> **Suggested header image:** Illustration of a dark, lonely planet drifting in front of a distant star field, with one star appearing brighter where the planet's path crosses it.
> **Alt text:** "Illustration: a dark planet with no sun drifts in front of distant stars. One background star looks brighter because the planet's gravity bends and focuses its light."

**Evidence level:** 🟢 **Level 1 — Confirmed** that microlensing detects planets, including free-floating planet candidates. 🔵 **Level 2 — Strong evidence** about how common Earth-mass free-floating planets are; the numbers are still being refined.

---

Take a clear drinking glass and look at a dot on a piece of paper through its thick, round bottom. Slide the glass slowly across the dot. For a moment, the dot looks bigger and brighter. Then it goes back to normal.

Gravity can do something like that with light.

## Gravity bends light

More than a hundred years ago, Albert Einstein's theory of gravity predicted that heavy objects bend the path of light. This was first tested during a solar eclipse in 1919, when stars near the Sun appeared slightly shifted.

When a star or planet passes almost exactly in front of a more distant star, its gravity acts like a weak magnifying glass. The background star gets brighter for a while, then fades back. This is called **microlensing**.

## Small mass, short flash

Here is the key: **the lighter the object, the shorter the brightening.**

- A star passing in front can cause a brightening that lasts weeks.
- A Jupiter-mass planet: about a day or two.
- An Earth-mass planet: just hours — or less.

So if you want to find small planets drifting alone, you need to watch millions of stars **very often**.

## Lonely planets are real

Survey teams like **OGLE** (Poland) and **KMTNet** (South Korea) watch huge numbers of stars near the center of our galaxy, often many times per night. In 2020, Przemek Mróz and colleagues reported an event called **OGLE-2016-BLG-1928**. Its brightening lasted only about **42 minutes** on its main timescale — the shortest of its kind found at that time. The best explanation is a **free-floating planet** about the mass of Earth, or a planet very far from any star.

Other studies, such as from the Japanese–New Zealand **MOA** survey, have suggested that rogue planets could be very common — perhaps even more common than stars. Those estimates are still being refined.

## A new telescope is built for this

NASA's **Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope** is designed to watch the crowded center of our galaxy from space, without Earth's atmosphere or daylight getting in the way. Scientists expect it to find many free-floating planets, including small ones. That will turn today's estimates into much better measurements.

## What is still open

> How common are Earth-mass and smaller planets drifting between the stars, and where did they come from?

Most were probably flung out of their home systems when young planets pushed each other around. Counting them tells us how violent planet formation can be.

## How to tell a real lensing flash from a fake

1. **Same in every color.** Gravity bends all colors the same way. A true lensing flash brightens a star equally in red and blue light. A stellar flare is usually much stronger in blue.
2. **The right shape.** A simple lensing event rises and falls smoothly and symmetrically.
3. **It happens once.** The chance of the same alignment happening again is tiny. Repeating flashes are usually something else.
4. **Enough data points.** Very short events need many measurements during the flash.

## How we could be wrong

- Some short brightenings are flares from small stars.
- Some are glitches, cosmic-ray hits, or moving asteroids.
- A "free-floating" planet might actually have a star very far away that we can't detect yet.
