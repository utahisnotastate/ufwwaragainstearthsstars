# Finding Caves on the Moon Without Going Inside

**Spacecraft can "feel" what is under a planet's surface by measuring tiny changes in gravity. Scientists have used this — and radar — to find signs of lava tubes on the Moon. More may be waiting in the data.**

> **Alt text:** "Illustration: a cut-away of the Moon's surface with a pit leading into an underground lava tube. Two spacecraft fly overhead, measuring gravity. Empty tunnels slightly change the gravity above them."

Have you ever knocked on a wall to find a hollow spot? Or tapped a watermelon to see if it is ripe? The sound changes depending on what's inside.

Spacecraft can do something like that with gravity. Rock is heavy. Empty space is not. So a big empty tunnel underground makes gravity just a tiny bit weaker right above it. A spacecraft flying over feels that tiny difference.

## Two spacecraft playing catch

NASA's **GRAIL** mission sent two small spacecraft, nicknamed **Ebb and Flow**, to the Moon in 2011–2012. They flew one behind the other. As they passed over heavier or lighter areas, the front one sped up or slowed down a little before the back one did. By measuring the distance between them extremely precisely, scientists made the most detailed gravity map of the Moon ever made.

## Lava tubes on the Moon

Long ago, rivers of lava flowed across the Moon. On Earth, flowing lava can form tunnels called **lava tubes**: the surface cools and hardens while hot lava keeps flowing underneath, then drains away, leaving a hollow tube.

In 2009, Japan's **Kaguya** spacecraft photographed a deep, round **pit** in the Moon's Marius Hills region — a hole that might be a skylight into a tube. Many more pits have been found since.

In 2017, a team led by Loïc Chappaz reported gravity signals in GRAIL data that fit what large empty lava tubes would produce under the Marius Hills area.

In 2024, a team led by Leonardo Carrer published radar evidence in *Nature Astronomy* that a pit in **Mare Tranquillitatis** — not far from where Apollo 11 landed — opens into a cave passage extending tens of meters underground. They used radar data from NASA's Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter. It is one of the strongest signs yet of an accessible cave on the Moon.

Caves like these could one day protect astronauts from radiation, extreme temperatures and tiny meteorites.

## Why maps can look smooth

Making a global gravity map means combining a huge number of measurements. Some areas have better coverage than others, and the data has noise. To keep the map from showing fake bumps, scientists smooth it in careful ways. This is good practice — it is not hiding anything. But it means very small, sharp features can be blurred in the global map.

Scientists also use **local** methods that look closely at small regions and at the raw tracking data. That is where small tunnels might show up.

## What is still open

> Can careful local analysis of raw spacecraft tracking data reveal more lava tubes and buried dense masses on the Moon, Mars and other worlds?

## How to test it

1. Pick a region with known pits or volcanic features.
2. Use public raw tracking data and gravity products from NASA's Planetary Data System.
3. Model what an empty tube of a given size and depth would produce.
4. Compare with the measured signal along each spacecraft pass.
5. Check against other data: radar, surface images, and topography.

## How we could be wrong

- Many things change gravity slightly: lighter rocks, buried craters, and changes in crust thickness.
- Noise in the tracking data can create small fake signals.
- A small tube deep underground may produce a signal too weak to detect.

A strong case needs several kinds of evidence pointing to the same place, like a gravity dip, a surface pit and a radar echo.

## How you can help

- **Kids and families:** Knock along a wall at home. Can you hear where the hollow parts are? (Ask an adult first!) That is a sound version of what GRAIL did with gravity.
- **Students:** Read about GRAIL and the 2024 lunar cave study. Write a short summary of how each method works.
- **Data fans:** Explore lunar data in NASA's Planetary Data System and the Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter image archive. Pick a pit and learn everything you can about it.
