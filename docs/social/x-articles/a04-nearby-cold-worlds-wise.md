# Cold, Dark Neighbors Hiding in Heat-Vision Maps of the Sky

**Some of the Sun's nearest neighbors are too cold to shine like stars. Infrared surveys and volunteers have found many of them. A few more may still be moving, unnoticed, through the data.**

> **Suggested header image:** Two infrared sky images side by side, taken years apart, with a small red dot that has moved between them, circled.
> **Alt text:** "Two infrared images of the same patch of sky, taken years apart. One faint dot has moved. A moving dot like this can be a cold nearby object."

**Evidence level:** 🟢 **Level 1 — Confirmed** that cold brown dwarfs near the Sun have been found in WISE data, including by volunteers. 🟡 **Level 3 — Testable idea** that more are still waiting. 🟠 **Level 4 — Long shot** that any are closer to us than the nearest star, Proxima Centauri.

---

Hold your thumb up at arm's length. Close your left eye. Now open it and close your right eye. Your thumb seems to jump!

Now do the same with something far away, like a tree across the street. It barely moves.

**Close things jump more.** Astronomers use this simple trick to find the Sun's closest neighbors. As Earth goes around the Sun, nearby objects seem to shift back and forth in the sky. They also drift steadily across the sky over the years. The closer they are, the bigger these motions.

## Failed stars that glow in heat vision

A **brown dwarf** is an object bigger than a planet but too small to burn like a star. People sometimes call them "failed stars." They start out warm and slowly cool down for billions of years. Old ones are very cold and very dim in normal light. But they still glow faintly in **infrared** — a kind of light our eyes can't see, often called "heat vision."

NASA's **WISE** spacecraft, launched in 2009, mapped the whole sky in infrared. Later, as **NEOWISE**, it kept scanning the sky until 2024. Together they made images of the whole sky many times over more than ten years.

## Neighbors already found

These maps have already revealed close neighbors that no one had seen before:

- **Luhman 16 AB** — a pair of brown dwarfs about 6.5 light-years away, found in 2013. It is one of the closest systems to the Sun.
- **WISE 0855−0714** — found in 2014, about 7.4 light-years away. It is one of the coldest objects ever found outside our Solar System, with a temperature not far from Earth's own.

And volunteers have helped a lot. Since 2017, people in the **Backyard Worlds: Planet 9** citizen-science project have flipped through WISE images looking for moving dots. They have helped discover more than a thousand brown dwarfs and other cool objects near the Sun.

## What is still open

> Are there still undiscovered cold brown dwarfs, or even free-floating planets, near the Sun, hidden as faint moving dots in WISE and NEOWISE data?

Very cold, very faint objects are the hardest to catch. Fast-moving ones can also be tricky. When an object moves a lot between images, it can be harder to match its separate sightings together.

## How to test it

1. Take repeated infrared images of the same patch of sky from different years.
2. Look for faint dots that move along a straight line **plus** a yearly back-and-forth wiggle.
3. Check their colors. The coldest brown dwarfs are much brighter at longer infrared wavelengths.
4. Fit five numbers to the positions: where it is, how fast it drifts (two numbers), and how big its yearly wiggle is. The wiggle size tells you the distance.
5. Rule out other look-alikes: image artifacts, asteroids, and distant objects with odd colors.

Our free `fdastro.astrometry` code and Tutorial 6 show how to do this fit.

## How we could be wrong

- Many "moving dots" are image artifacts, especially near bright stars.
- Asteroids move too, but much faster and in a different pattern.
- Searches have already ruled out a Jupiter-sized object or bigger fairly close to the Sun. So any new neighbors are likely to be small, cold and faint.
- Something closer than Proxima Centauri (about 4.2 light-years) would probably have been noticed already, unless it is very small and cold. That is why we call it a long shot.

## How you can help

- **Kids and families:** Do the thumb-blink activity. Then try it with objects at different distances. Which ones jump the most?
- **Everyone:** Join **Backyard Worlds: Planet 9** on Zooniverse. You will flip through real WISE images and mark moving dots. Follow that project's rules for reporting what you find.
- **Coders:** Try Tutorial 6. Fake a nearby brown dwarf, add noise, and see if you can measure its distance.
