# Baby Planets Leave Wakes in Their Disks

**Young stars are wrapped in flat disks of gas and dust where planets are born. The ALMA telescope sees rings, gaps and tiny "kinks" in how the gas moves. Some of those kinks are the wakes of planets we can't see directly.**

> **Suggested header image:** Illustration of a young star surrounded by a flat, glowing disk with dark rings, and one small planet in a gap leaving a spiral wake.
> **Alt text:** "Illustration: a young star surrounded by a glowing disk with dark rings. A small planet in one gap leaves a curved wake in the gas, like a boat in water."

**Evidence level:** 🟢 **Level 1 — Confirmed** that young planets exist inside disks (for example, the PDS 70 system). 🔵 **Level 2 — Strong evidence** that velocity "kinks" seen by ALMA reveal hidden planets; each case still needs confirmation.

---

Drag your finger slowly through a tray of water. You can't see the finger from below, but you can see the ripples and the V-shaped wake it leaves behind. If someone hid the finger under a cloth, you could still tell where it was by looking at the water.

Astronomers are doing something similar with baby planets.

## Where planets are born

Young stars are surrounded by flat, spinning disks of gas and dust. Planets grow inside these disks, a bit like snowballs rolling and growing.

The **ALMA** telescope in Chile is an array of dozens of radio dishes working together. It can see the cold dust and gas in these disks. In 2014, it took a famous picture of a young star called **HL Tauri**, showing bright rings and dark gaps — like a cosmic target. Since then, ALMA has found rings and gaps in many disks.

## Gaps are clues, not proof

A growing planet can clear a gap in its disk. But other processes can also make rings and gaps, like changes in how dust sticks together or how ice forms at different distances. So a gap alone does not prove a planet is there.

## Planets seen directly

In a young system called **PDS 70**, astronomers have directly imaged two young giant planets inside a gap in the disk. This shows that planets really do sit inside these gaps. It is 🟢 **Level 1 — Confirmed**.

## Wakes in the gas

Here is the clever part. ALMA can also measure how fast the gas is moving, using the Doppler effect — the same effect that makes a passing siren change pitch.

In a smooth disk, the gas moves in neat circles. But a planet disturbs the gas around it. This makes a small, local **kink** in the pattern of gas speeds.

In 2018, Christophe Pinte and colleagues reported such a kink in the disk around the young star **HD 163296**. It matched what a planet about twice the mass of Jupiter would do, far out in the disk. In the same journal issue, Richard Teague and colleagues reported signs of two more hidden planets in the same disk using gas motions. Later studies found kink-like features in many other disks.

## What is still open

> Do ALMA gas-velocity measurements reveal more young planets than images alone can show — and can we confirm them?

Many disk observations are public in the **ALMA Science Archive** after their protected period ends. Some may hold kinks no one has fully studied yet.

## How to test it

1. Use public ALMA observations of gas (for example, carbon monoxide) in nearby young disks.
2. Model how the gas would move in a smooth disk.
3. Subtract that model from the data and look for leftover local kinks.
4. Check whether the kink lines up with a gap or a spiral in the dust.
5. Compare with planet models to estimate the mass and location.
6. Ask for direct imaging or more data to confirm.

## How we could be wrong

- Disks can have their own turbulence, warps and spirals that are not caused by planets.
- Noise in the data can make small kinks look real.
- Assuming the wrong shape or tilt for the disk can create fake patterns.

The strongest cases show a kink, a gap and a spiral all pointing to the same place.

## How you can help

- **Kids and families:** Do the finger-in-water activity. Then have a friend hide their finger under a floating sheet of plastic wrap. Can you still find it by its ripples?
- **Students:** Look up the famous HL Tauri image. Count the rings. Then read about why rings don't always mean planets.
- **Data fans:** Explore the ALMA Science Archive and see what kinds of disk data are public.
