# The Ghostly Dust Clouds Near the Moon

**There are two special "balance points" in the Moon's orbit. Faint dust clouds there have been reported for decades and studied with polarized light. Could anything solid be there too? Probably not — but here is how we would check.**

---

Imagine a merry-go-round spinning in a park. On the floor there are two special spots where, if you set down a balloon, it seems to ride along with the horses without rolling away. Dust that blows into those spots tends to swirl around there for a while.

Space has spots like that too. They are called **Lagrange points**.

## Balance points in space

When a small object moves near two big ones — like Earth and the Moon — there are five places where the pulls and the motion balance out. Two of them, called **L4** and **L5**, sit along the Moon's orbit: one 60 degrees ahead of the Moon, and one 60 degrees behind.

Around the Sun and Jupiter, those points hold thousands of asteroids, called **Trojans**. Earth also has a couple of Trojan asteroids at the Sun–Earth points. (We cover those in another disclosure.)

But the **Earth–Moon** L4 and L5 points are different. The Sun's strong pull keeps disturbing them. That makes it hard for anything to stay there for a long time.

## The Kordylewski clouds

In 1961, the Polish astronomer Kazimierz Kordylewski reported seeing very faint glowing patches near the Earth–Moon L5 point. For decades, people argued about whether they were real. They are extremely faint and hard to separate from other sky glow.

In 2018 and 2019, a team of Hungarian scientists — Judit Slíz-Balogh, András Barta and Gábor Horváth — published studies in *Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society*. First they modeled how dust could gather at these points. Then they photographed the L5 region through polarizing filters. Dust that scatters sunlight can make the light polarized in a particular pattern. They reported a pattern that fits a dust cloud. Later papers by the same group reported polarization evidence at L4 as well.

These results are exciting, but the clouds are faint and seem to change over time. More independent observations would help. That is why we label them 🔵 **Level 2 — Strong evidence**.

## Could something solid be hiding there?

This is the long-shot question:

> Could there be solid rocks, not just dust, near the Earth–Moon L4 and L5 points?

So far, searches have not found any. Because the Sun keeps disturbing these points, most models suggest that solid objects would not stay there for long. A dark rock a few meters across would also be very hard to see.

So we call this 🟠 **Level 4 — Long shot**. It is worth understanding, but it is unlikely.

## Don't mix these up

There are some real "extra companions" of Earth — just not at the Earth–Moon balance points:

- **2024 PT5** was a small asteroid that was temporarily captured by Earth's gravity for about two months in late 2024 — a brief "mini-moon."
- **Kamoʻoalewa** is a small asteroid that travels around the Sun close to Earth and seems to circle us from our point of view. It is called a **quasi-satellite**. China's Tianwen-2 mission is on its way to study it.

## How we could be wrong (about the dust)

- Sky glow from sunlight scattered by interplanetary dust (zodiacal light) can mimic faint patches.
- Earth's atmosphere and camera optics can add their own polarization.
- Faint clouds that change over time are hard to measure the same way twice.

## How you can help

- **Kids and families:** Watch dust in a sunbeam in a dark room. Notice how you only see it from certain angles. Faint things in space can be like that too.
- **Students:** Read the 2018 and 2019 papers' summaries. List the reasons the authors give for believing the signal is real. Then list what could fool them.
- **Amateur astronomers:** Plan — but think carefully before running — a polarization observation. Write down every false signal you would need to rule out first.
