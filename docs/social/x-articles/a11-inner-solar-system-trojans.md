# Asteroids That Share an Orbit With Earth

**Jupiter has thousands of "Trojan" asteroids traveling with it. Earth has only two known so far. Venus and Mars have a few companions too. Many more may be hiding near the Sun's glare, where telescopes have a hard time looking.**

> **Alt text:** "Diagram: Earth's orbit around the Sun, with shaded zones 60 degrees ahead of and behind Earth. A small asteroid sits in one zone. These are Earth's Trojan regions."

---

Imagine walking around a big circular track. Two friends join you. One stays about one-sixth of a lap ahead of you, and one stays about one-sixth of a lap behind. All of you keep walking together, lap after lap.

In space, some asteroids do this with planets. They share the planet's orbit, staying ahead of it or behind it. They are called **Trojan asteroids**, or more generally **co-orbitals**.

## Jupiter's huge crowd

Jupiter has more than ten thousand known Trojan asteroids, gathered in two big swarms ahead of and behind it. NASA's **Lucy** mission, launched in 2021, is on its way to visit several of them.

## Earth's small club

Earth has only **two** known Trojan asteroids:

- **2010 TK7**, found in data from NASA's WISE spacecraft and announced in 2011.
- **2020 XL5**, confirmed as an Earth Trojan in 2022.

Mars has several Trojans, including **5261 Eureka**, found in 1990. Venus has had temporary companions too, such as **2013 ND15**.

## Why are they hard to find?

Earth's Trojan regions sit about 60 degrees from the Sun in our sky. That means they are best seen near **twilight** — just after sunset or before sunrise — when the sky is still bright and objects are low near the horizon, where the air is thick and blurry.

For Venus companions, it is even harder. Venus's orbit is inside Earth's, so its companions always appear fairly close to the Sun in our sky.

Most big asteroid surveys look at dark sky, far from the Sun, where it is easiest to find things. That is a sensible choice — but it leaves the twilight zones less explored.

## Twilight hunters

Some teams have started searching close to the Sun on purpose. In 2020, the Zwicky Transient Facility found **2020 AV2**, the first asteroid known to orbit the Sun entirely inside the orbit of Venus. A twilight search using the Dark Energy Camera in Chile has also found asteroids very close to the Sun, including **2021 PH27**, which has one of the shortest known orbits around the Sun.

NASA's **NEO Surveyor**, an infrared space telescope planned for launch around 2027, is designed to look for near-Earth asteroids, including in sky regions closer to the Sun.

## What is still open

> How many asteroids share orbits with Venus, Earth and Mars, and how many are waiting in twilight sky regions?

Every new one helps us understand how the inner Solar System formed and changed over time.

## How to test it

1. Use public asteroid orbit lists, such as NASA JPL's Small-Body Database.
2. Look for objects with nearly the same average orbit size as a planet.
3. Run a simple simulation to see whether they stay near the planet's Trojan zones over time.
4. Check where new candidates would appear in the sky, and when, to plan twilight observations.

Our `fdastro.kepler` code can help compute where objects are in their orbits.

## How we could be wrong

- Many objects visit Trojan zones only **temporarily**, then drift away. They are interesting, but not permanent members.
- Objects near the Sun are hard to measure, so early orbits can be uncertain.
- Models of how many should exist depend on assumptions about the early Solar System.

## How you can help

- **Kids and families:** Walk in a circle with two friends. Keep one friend always a few steps ahead of you and one always behind. That is how Trojans move!
- **Students:** Make a list of Earth's and Mars's known Trojans and the year each was found. Why do you think there are so few?
- **Amateur astronomers:** Learn how twilight observing works and why it is hard. Safety first: never point a telescope near the Sun without proper training and certified solar filters.
