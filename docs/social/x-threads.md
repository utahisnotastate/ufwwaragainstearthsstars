## Thread 1 — D14 Interstellar visitors in unlinked asteroid data (12 Jan 2027)

1/7 Three objects from other star systems have been found passing through ours: ʻOumuamua (2017), Borisov (2019) and 3I/ATLAS (2025). Could another be hiding in lists of unconnected asteroid sightings? ☄️

2/7 How do we know they're visitors? Their paths are open curves, not closed loops. Orbit "eccentricity" above 1 means the Sun can't hold on. 3I/ATLAS: about 6.1. That's very open.

3/7 Far from the Sun, ʻOumuamua moves about 26 km/s, Borisov about 32 km/s, 3I/ATLAS about 58 km/s. Fast enough to cross the US in a couple of minutes.

4/7 Surveys see a moving dot a few times per night: a "tracklet." Connecting tracklets across nights is a giant puzzle. Sightings not yet connected go into a public Minor Planet Center list.

5/7 In 2018, a method called HelioLinC connected many of those leftover sightings into new asteroid orbits. So the list holds real objects. Are any interstellar? That's our Level 3 — Testable idea.

6/7 How to test: fit an orbit without assuming it's a closed loop, compare the best closed and best open fits, rule out known objects, have someone repeat it, report to the MPC first.

7/7 Kids: draw an ellipse with two pins and string, then an open curve. Which is the visitor? Full report, code and tutorial: [LINK]

## Thread 2 — D02 Planets whose transits fade away (19 Jan 2027)

1/6 A planet stopped crossing in front of its star. It didn't vanish. Its orbit tilted. 🪐

2/6 TOI-2535 b is a hot giant planet about 1,000 light-years away. NASA's TESS watched it 2019–2026. Its transits got shorter and shallower, then were gone in March 2026.

3/6 A UCLA-led team (Yee et al., ApJ Letters, Nov 2026) says the orbit is slowly tipping, likely pulled by an unseen neighbor at least as heavy as Saturn. It may transit again in a few decades.

4/6 That tilting is Level 1 — Confirmed physics, seen in published studies. Our question: how many other planets in TESS and Kepler data have transits slowly fading? Level 3 — Testable idea.

5/6 To test: measure each transit's depth and length across years. Look for a steady trend in both. Rule out nearby stars blending in, star spots and processing differences.

6/6 Kids: swing a ball on a string around a lamp so its shadow hits a wall. Slowly tilt the circle. Watch the shadow miss! Full report and Tutorial 7: [LINK]

## Thread 3 — D01 Unseen planets in the far Solar System (26 Jan 2027)

1/6 In 1846, Neptune was found with math before anyone saw it. Uranus wasn't moving as expected, so astronomers calculated where an unseen planet must be. Could it happen again? 🔭

2/6 Far beyond Neptune, some icy objects have long, stretched orbits that seem to line up. In 2016, astronomers suggested an unseen "Planet Nine" might shape them.

3/6 Others think the lining-up may come from where telescopes happened to look. Healthy debate! So: Level 3 — Testable idea. Not proven, not ruled out.

4/6 Spacecraft tracking and pulsar "clocks" feel tiny tugs too. They're great at showing where a big planet ISN'T. Each limit shrinks the search area.

5/6 Rubin Observatory reported a big distant object, 2026 ED138, in Oct 2026: roughly 600–1,500 km across, ~46 times farther than Earth from the Sun. Not Planet Nine, but proof of a powerful new survey.

6/6 Kids: hide a heavy ball under a stretched sheet and roll marbles across. Can a friend find the ball by watching marbles curve? Full report: [LINK]

## Thread 4 — D07 Free-floating planets in brief lensing flashes (2 Feb 2027)

1/6 Some planets have no star. They drift alone. We can't see them, but we can catch them magnifying the light of a star behind them. 🌌

2/6 Einstein predicted gravity bends light. When an object passes almost exactly in front of a distant star, the star brightens, then fades. That's microlensing.

3/6 Lighter object = shorter flash. A star: weeks. A Jupiter: about a day or two. An Earth: hours or less.

4/6 In 2020, OGLE and KMTNet data revealed a flash with a timescale of about 42 minutes, likely an Earth-mass rogue planet. Microlensing planets: Level 1 — Confirmed. How common rogue Earths are: Level 2.

5/6 Real lensing brightens all colors equally. Flares from stars are usually much bluer. That's one way to spot fakes.

6/6 Kids: look at a dot through the thick bottom of a clear glass and slide it past. That's lensing! Try Tutorial 10 to calculate flash lengths: [LINK]

## Thread 5 — D04 Cold, dark neighbors in infrared sky maps (9 Feb 2027)

1/6 Hold up your thumb, blink one eye, then the other. It jumps! Close things jump more. Astronomers use this to find the Sun's nearest neighbors. ⭐

2/6 Brown dwarfs are "failed stars": too small to burn like the Sun. Old ones are cold and dim, but glow in infrared ("heat vision").

3/6 NASA's WISE spacecraft mapped the whole sky in infrared, then kept scanning as NEOWISE until 2024. It revealed neighbors like Luhman 16 AB and the very cold WISE 0855−0714.

4/6 Volunteers on Backyard Worlds: Planet 9 have helped find more than a thousand cool objects near the Sun. Citizen science works!

5/6 Are there still undiscovered cold neighbors? Level 3 — Testable idea. Closer than Proxima Centauri? Level 4 — Long shot; searches already rule out big ones nearby.

6/6 Try the thumb-blink activity, join Backyard Worlds on Zooniverse, or fit distances yourself with Tutorial 6: [LINK]

## Thread 6 — D15 Captured interstellar objects already orbiting the Sun (16 Feb 2027)

1/6 Nearly everything orbits the Sun the same way, because the Solar System formed from one spinning disk. So what about the few things going backwards? ☄️

2/6 Asteroid 2015 BZ509 (Kaʻepaokaʻawela) shares Jupiter's path but travels the opposite way. Its orbit is tilted about 163°.

3/6 A 2018 study suggested it has been there since the Solar System was young, and may have been captured from another star. Others question this. Level 3 — Testable idea.

4/6 Important: backwards doesn't automatically mean "from another star." Halley's Comet orbits backwards and it's one of ours.

5/6 A number called the Tisserand parameter helps sort orbits. For 2015 BZ509 we calculate about −0.75. Negative values often mean backwards-moving orbits.

6/6 Kids: walk in a circle with friends while one "visitor" walks the other way. Try Tutorial 5 to compute the Tisserand parameter: [LINK]

## Thread 7 — D03 Planets seen only once (23 Feb 2027)

1/5 TESS watches each patch of sky for about 27 days. A planet with a 300-day orbit might cross its star just once in that time. One dip. That's it. 🪐

2/5 These long-period planets are cooler and more like our own Solar System's planets. They're important, and hard to confirm.

3/5 A single dip could also be two stars eclipsing, a spacecraft glitch, scattered light or an asteroid. Every one needs careful checks.

4/5 Single-transit planets exist: Level 1 — Confirmed. Many more waiting in public data: Level 3 — Testable idea. Volunteers on Planet Hunters TESS look for them by eye.

5/5 Kids: hold up a card for one second during a story. Did everyone see it? Grown-ups: join Planet Hunters TESS or try Tutorial 7: [LINK]

## Thread 8 — D09 Baby planets hidden in dusty disks (2 Mar 2027)

1/6 Drag a finger through water and you see a wake, even if the finger is hidden. Young planets leave wakes too. 🌌

2/6 Young stars have flat disks of gas and dust where planets form. The ALMA telescope in Chile sees rings and gaps in them, like the famous 2014 image of HL Tauri.

3/6 Gaps alone don't prove planets; other processes make rings too. But in the PDS 70 system, young planets have been seen directly inside a gap. Level 1 — Confirmed.

4/6 ALMA also measures gas speeds. A planet makes a small "kink" in the pattern. In 2018, kinks in the disk of HD 163296 pointed to hidden planets. Level 2 — Strong evidence.

5/6 Our question: do public ALMA data hold more kinks than anyone has studied? Disks can have turbulence and warps, so careful modeling matters.

6/6 Kids: hide your finger under plastic wrap on water and move it. Can a friend track it by the ripples? Full report: [LINK]

## Thread 9 — D11 Trojan and co-orbital asteroids near Venus, Earth and Mars (9 Mar 2027)

1/6 Jupiter travels with over ten thousand "Trojan" asteroids, sharing its orbit. Earth has only two known. Why so few? 🌍

2/6 Earth's Trojans: 2010 TK7 (found in WISE data) and 2020 XL5 (confirmed in 2022). Mars has several, like 5261 Eureka. Venus has had temporary companions.

3/6 Earth's Trojan zones sit about 60° from the Sun in our sky, best seen in twilight, low in bright sky. Hard places to search.

4/6 Twilight hunters are finding things: 2020 AV2 was the first asteroid known to orbit entirely inside Venus's orbit.

5/6 Our question: how many more share orbits with Venus, Earth and Mars? Level 3 — Testable idea. Many co-orbitals are temporary visitors, which is interesting too.

6/6 Kids: walk a circle with two friends, one always ahead, one always behind. That's a Trojan! Never point telescopes near the Sun. Full report: [LINK]

## Thread 10 — D12 Exomoons from timing and duration wobbles (16 Mar 2027)

1/6 Our Solar System has hundreds of moons. Around other stars: not one confirmed yet. 🌙

2/6 Two candidates, Kepler-1625 b-i and Kepler-1708 b-i, were reported in 2018 and 2022. A 2024 reanalysis argued against them; the original team says they remain possible. Science at work!

3/6 A moon tugs its planet. The planet arrives a bit early or late (timing), and crosses a bit faster or slower (duration).

4/6 For a moon, those two patterns peak a quarter-cycle apart. Plot one against the other and you get a loop. A neat clue, proposed in 2009.

5/6 Our question: do any known planets show that quarter-step pattern? Level 3 — Testable idea. Star spots, other planets and noise can fool us, so we use false-alarm tests.

6/6 Kids: run laps while a friend swings around you holding your hand. Time every lap! Coders: try Tutorial 8: [LINK]

## Thread 11 — D13 Tilted orbits that point to unseen planets (23 Mar 2027)

1/6 Some planets orbit their stars at steep angles, even over the poles. Something tipped them. What? 🪐

2/6 When a planet crosses a spinning star, it blocks the side coming toward us, then the side going away. That shifts the starlight in a pattern that reveals the tilt.

3/6 Many planets are tilted: Level 1 — Confirmed. HAT-P-11 b orbits nearly over its star's poles, and a big outer planet, HAT-P-11 c, was found in 2018.

4/6 Slow tugs make orbits wobble like a spinning top. When two wobbles match speed, small pushes add up to big tilts. Outer planets as the cause: Level 2.

5/6 Our question: can a tilted planet tell us what outer planet to look for, and where? Level 3. Gaia's new data release is a fresh place to check.

6/6 Kids: spin a top and tap it gently. Watch the slow wobble. Coders: Tutorial 9 makes a search map: [LINK]

## Thread 12 — D10 Caves and buried masses mapped by gravity (30 Mar 2027)

1/6 Knock on a wall and you can hear the hollow spots. Spacecraft can "feel" hollow spots under a planet's surface using gravity. 🌙

2/6 NASA's GRAIL mission (2011–2012) flew two spacecraft, Ebb and Flow, around the Moon, measuring the distance between them very precisely. Result: the best map of lunar gravity ever.

3/6 The Moon has deep pits, some possibly skylights into old lava tubes. A 2017 study found GRAIL signals that fit buried empty tubes.

4/6 In 2024, radar data from NASA's Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter showed a pit in Mare Tranquillitatis likely opens into a cave passage. Level 2 — Strong evidence.

5/6 Our question: can careful local study of raw tracking data reveal more tubes and buried masses? Level 3 — Testable idea. Global maps are smoothed for good reasons; local methods dig deeper.

6/6 Kids: with a grown-up's OK, knock along a wall to find hollow spots. Full report: [LINK]

## Thread 13 — D08 Radio auroras from planets and brown dwarfs (6 Apr 2027)

1/6 Auroras don't just glow. They also give off radio waves. Jupiter's are so strong you can pick them up with a simple antenna. 📡

2/6 Jupiter's radio bursts were discovered in 1955. Spacecraft found Earth's auroras shine in radio too, though our upper atmosphere blocks those waves from the ground.

3/6 In 2015, a brown dwarf was shown to have auroras far stronger than Jupiter's. In 2020, a LOFAR team even found a cold brown dwarf by its radio signal first.

4/6 Also in 2020, radio waves from the red dwarf GJ 1151 looked like a planet stirring its star's magnetic field. Level 2 — Strong evidence; the planet is not yet found.

5/6 Our question: do public radio surveys hold auroral signals from unknown brown dwarfs or planets? Level 3. Human radio interference is the big challenge.

6/6 Kids: listen to Jupiter radio recordings, then the hiss between AM stations. Students: look up NASA's Radio JOVE kits. Full report: [LINK]

## Thread 14 — D06 Dust clouds and possible mini-moons at the Earth–Moon balance points (13 Apr 2027)

1/6 Along the Moon's orbit are two "balance points," 60° ahead of and behind the Moon, called L4 and L5. Something faint may linger there. 🌙

2/6 In 1961, astronomer Kazimierz Kordylewski reported faint glowing clouds near these points. For decades, people argued whether they were real.

3/6 Studies published in 2018–2019, and later, used polarized light to report dust clouds at L5 and then L4. Level 2 — Strong evidence; more independent checks would help.

4/6 Solid mini-moons parked there? Searches haven't found any, and the Sun's pull makes long stays unlikely. Level 4 — Long shot.

5/6 Don't mix up: 2024 PT5 was briefly captured by Earth in 2024, and Kamoʻoalewa is a "quasi-satellite" circling the Sun near Earth. Neither sits at the Earth–Moon points.

6/6 Kids: watch dust in a sunbeam. You see it only from certain angles. Faint space clouds are like that. Full report: [LINK]

## Thread 15 — D05 Polarized light from planets (20 Apr 2027)

1/6 Tilt your head while wearing polarized sunglasses and looking at a phone. It goes dark! Light can "wiggle" in a direction. That's polarization. 🔭

2/6 Starlight mostly wiggles every which way. Light bouncing off a planet's air or clouds can wiggle more in one direction. In principle, that could help pick out a planet.

3/6 But planets are faint. A giant planet close to its star reflects only parts per million of the star's light. An Earth twin: about one ten-billionth.

4/6 Big telescopes use polarization beautifully, for example to image dusty disks: Level 1 — Confirmed. Backyard exoplanet polarization: Level 4 — Long shot. Our air and optics add bigger effects than the signal.

5/6 Students: try making a "noise budget." How small a signal could a small telescope detect in one night? Working out "not yet" is real science.

6/6 Kids: look at the blue sky (never the Sun!) through polarized sunglasses and tilt your head. Full report: [LINK]

## Kid fact posts

Each is posted on the Wednesday after the matching disclosure. All are under 280 characters.

1. Kid fact: An interstellar object is a rock or comet from another star system just passing through ours. Only three have been found so far! ☄️ #KidsAstronomy
2. Kid fact: When a planet passes in front of its star, it blocks a tiny bit of light. Astronomers call this a transit. That's how TESS finds planets! 🪐
3. Kid fact: Neptune was found in 1846 using math first. Astronomers saw Uranus being tugged and worked out where the hidden planet must be. 🔭
4. Kid fact: Some planets have no star at all. They drift alone through the galaxy. Scientists call them free-floating or rogue planets. 🌌
5. Kid fact: Brown dwarfs are too big to be planets and too small to shine like the Sun. Old ones are so cold they glow mostly in infrared, or "heat vision." ⭐
6. Kid fact: Almost everything in our Solar System orbits the Sun the same way. Halley's Comet is a rebel: it goes the opposite way! ☄️
7. Kid fact: Close things seem to jump more when you blink one eye, then the other. That's parallax, and it's how astronomers measure distances to nearby stars. 👀
8. Kid fact: Saturn has more than two hundred known moons. But no moon has been confirmed yet around a planet outside our Solar System. 🌙
9. Kid fact: Jupiter sends out radio waves from its auroras. With a simple antenna, students can listen to them from Earth! 📡
10. Kid fact: The Moon has deep pits that may lead to underground lava tubes. Future astronauts might use caves like these for shelter. 🌙

## Myth vs. fact posts

Each is posted on the Wednesday after the matching disclosure, or when the myth comes up in replies.

1. Myth: "Disclosure" means someone is hiding space secrets. Fact: Our disclosures are honest reports about public data that hasn't been fully studied yet. Nothing secret, just a lot of data. 🔭
2. Myth: Interstellar objects must be alien spacecraft. Fact: ʻOumuamua, Borisov and 3I/ATLAS have all been studied as natural objects. A real claim of alien technology would need many independent checks. ☄️
3. Myth: If a planet's transits stop, the planet was destroyed. Fact: Usually its orbit just tilted so it no longer crosses its star from our view. TOI-2535 b is still there. 🪐
4. Myth: Planet Nine has been found. Fact: Not as of today. It's a debated idea (Level 3 — Testable idea). New surveys like Rubin's are helping test it. 🔭
5. Myth: Anything orbiting backwards must come from another star. Fact: Halley's Comet goes backwards and it's from our own Solar System. Backwards is a clue, not proof. ☄️
6. Myth: Planets always orbit stars. Fact: Microlensing surveys have found planets drifting alone, with no star nearby. 🌌
7. Myth: A bigger backyard telescope can photograph Earth-like planets around other stars. Fact: The planet would be about ten billion times fainter than its star, right next to it. That needs giant, special telescopes. 🔭
8. Myth: Scientists ignore strange data. Fact: Teams have limited time and telescopes, so they prioritize. Public archives let everyone help look at the rest. 📡
9. Myth: Exomoons have been confirmed. Fact: Not yet. Two candidates are being debated, and a timing test may help find more. 🌙
10. Myth: Rings in a young star's disk always mean planets. Fact: Planets can make gaps, but other processes can too. Gas "kinks" and direct images give stronger evidence. 🌌
