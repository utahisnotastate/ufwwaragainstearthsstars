# Lesson 6 — Backwards Orbits

| | |
|---|---|
| **Ages** | 9–13 |
| **Time** | 40 minutes |
| **Group size** | Small groups (water demo) and whole class (walking) |
| **Connects to** | [D15 — Captured interstellar objects already orbiting the Sun](../disclosures/d15-captured-interstellar-objects.md) |

## Big question

**Why do almost all planets go around the Sun the same way — and what does it mean when something goes backwards?**

## For young readers

Think of cars going around a roundabout. They all go the same way. If you saw one car going the other way, you would ask, "Where did that car come from?"

All eight planets go around the Sun in the same direction. That is because they were born from one big spinning disk of gas and dust, long ago.

But a few small objects go around **backwards**. Scientists call this **retrograde**. Some of them may have been knocked into backwards paths. A few scientists think one or two might even have come from another star and been captured! That idea is still being tested.

## Learning goals

Learners will be able to:

1. Explain that the planets formed from one spinning disk, so they orbit the same way.
2. Define "retrograde orbit."
3. Give two possible explanations for a backwards orbit.

## NGSS alignment

- **MS-ESS1-2** — gravity and the formation and motion of the Solar System.

## Materials

- A large clear bowl of water
- Ground pepper, glitter or small paper bits
- A spoon
- Tape or chalk for a floor circle

## Safety

- Wipe up spilled water right away.
- Walking activity: walk slowly, use two separate lanes (inner and outer) so no one bumps.

## Steps

### Part A — The spinning disk

1. Sprinkle pepper on the water.
2. Stir the water in a circle a few times, then pull the spoon out.
3. Watch. Which way do the pepper bits go? (All the same way.) This is like the young Solar System's spinning disk. Things that formed from it share its direction.

### Part B — The roundabout

4. Make a big circle on the floor with an inner lane and an outer lane.
5. Most learners walk slowly counter-clockwise in the inner lane. These are planets and asteroids.
6. One learner walks clockwise in the outer lane. This is a backwards (retrograde) object.
7. Ask the class: "Would the backwards walker have started that way if everyone started from the same spinning group?"

### Part C — Detective thinking

8. In groups, list ways an object could end up going backwards. Then compare with the list in "For the teacher."

**Accessible version:** Use a turntable or a round tray with small magnets or checkers moving in one direction, and one moving the other way, for learners to feel.

## Discussion questions

1. Why do the planets all go the same way?
2. Halley's Comet goes around the Sun backwards. Does that mean it came from another star? (Not necessarily — comets can be thrown into many directions by the giant planets.)
3. Neptune's big moon Triton goes around Neptune backwards. What might that tell us about where Triton came from?
4. What evidence would help prove an object came from another star?

## Check for understanding (answers for the teacher)

| Question | Expected answer |
|---|---|
| What does "retrograde orbit" mean? | Orbiting in the opposite direction to the planets. |
| Why do planets share one direction? | They formed from the same spinning disk. |
| Two possible reasons for a backwards orbit? | (1) It was pushed into a new path by a close pass near a big planet; (2) it was captured from somewhere else, such as another star's system, long ago. |
| Is a backwards orbit proof of interstellar origin? | No. It is a clue, not proof. |

## Extension

- Older learners: the "Tisserand parameter" is a number astronomers use to sort orbits. Objects with negative values orbit backwards relative to Jupiter. See [Tutorial 5](../tutorials/t05-tisserand-and-backwards-orbits.md).
- Research the asteroid **Kaʻepaokaʻawela**. Its Hawaiian name means roughly "the mischievous opposite-moving companion of Jupiter."

## For the teacher: the science behind it

**Disk origin (🟢 Level 1).** The Solar System formed from a rotating protoplanetary disk about 4.6 billion years ago, so planets orbit prograde and roughly in one plane. Most asteroids share this.

**Retrograde orbits (🟢 Level 1).** An orbit with inclination *i* > 90° relative to the ecliptic is retrograde. Examples: comet 1P/Halley (*i* ≈ 162°); Neptune's moon Triton (retrograde around Neptune, widely thought to be a captured Kuiper Belt object); asteroid (514107) Kaʻepaokaʻawela, 2015 BZ509, a retrograde co-orbital of Jupiter (*i* ≈ 163°; Wiegert et al. 2017). Some trans-Neptunian objects have near-polar or retrograde orbits, such as 2008 KV42 ("Drac", *i* ≈ 103°) and 2011 KT19 ("Niku", now officially named 471325 Taowu, *i* ≈ 110°).

**Where they come from.** Common explanations: scattering by giant planets, the Kozai–Lidov mechanism, and delivery from the Oort Cloud. Namouni & Morais (2018) argued that Kaʻepaokaʻawela's orbit has been stable since the Solar System's birth and may therefore be a captured interstellar object (🟡 Level 3 — Testable idea). Other studies dispute this, so the question remains open. See [D15](../disclosures/d15-captured-interstellar-objects.md).

**Tisserand parameter.** *T*ⱼ = *a*ⱼ/*a* + 2 cos *i* √[(*a*/*a*ⱼ)(1 − *e*²)]. For Kaʻepaokaʻawela, with *a* ≈ 5.14 au, *e* ≈ 0.38 and *i* ≈ 163°, *T*ⱼ ≈ −0.7.
