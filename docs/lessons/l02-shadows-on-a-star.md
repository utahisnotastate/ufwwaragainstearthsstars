# Lesson 2 — Shadows on a Star

| | |
|---|---|
| **Ages** | 8–12 |
| **Time** | 40–50 minutes |
| **Group size** | 3–4 learners per lamp |
| **Connects to** | [D02 — Planets whose transits fade away](../disclosures/d02-vanishing-transits.md) and [D03 — Planets seen only once](../disclosures/d03-single-transit-planets.md) |

## Big question

**How can we find a planet around another star when the planet is too small and dim to see?**

## For young readers

Stars are very far away. Their planets are tiny and dark next to them. But when a planet passes in front of its star, it blocks a little bit of the star's light. The star gets a tiny bit dimmer. This is called a **transit**.

Space telescopes like TESS watch thousands of stars and look for these tiny dips. A bigger planet makes a bigger dip.

Sometimes a planet's path tilts a little bit each year. Then it stops passing in front of the star. Its dips get smaller and then disappear! In 2026, scientists saw this happen to a planet called TOI-2535 b.

## Learning goals

Learners will be able to:

1. Explain that a transit is a small dip in a star's brightness.
2. Show that bigger planets block more light.
3. Explain why a tilted orbit can stop a planet from transiting.

## NGSS alignment

- **5-ESS1-1** — star brightness as seen from Earth.
- **4-PS4-2** — light reaching the eye lets us see objects.

## Materials

- A lamp with a round bulb or frosted globe (LED preferred) — the "star"
- Balls of different sizes on sticks (for example, a ping-pong ball, a marble and a bead on a pencil or skewer) — the "planets"
- Optional: a phone with a free light-meter app (for example, the free *phyphox* app has a light sensor tool on many phones)
- Paper and pencils for graphing

## Safety

- Use an LED lamp. Do not touch bulbs; some get hot.
- Skewers can be sharp: tape over the tips.
- Never point a phone or eyes at the real Sun.

## Steps

1. **Set the scene.** Darken the room. Put the lamp on a table. One learner sits about 2–3 m away at lamp height — they are the "telescope".
2. **Make a transit.** A second learner moves the small ball on a stick slowly across the front of the lamp, at the telescope's eye level.
3. **Watch the light.** Ask the telescope learner: "Did the star get dimmer?" With the light-meter app placed where the telescope learner's eyes were, read the numbers before, during and after the ball passes.
4. **Graph it.** Draw time across the bottom and brightness up the side. You should see a flat line, a dip, and a flat line again. This shape is a **light curve**.
5. **Try a bigger planet.** Repeat with the ping-pong ball. Is the dip deeper? (Yes.)
6. **Try a tilted path.** Move the ball across the lamp a bit **higher** each time. First the dip gets smaller (the ball only covers the edge — a **grazing** transit). Then the ball passes above the lamp and there is no dip at all. This is what happened to TOI-2535 b.
7. **Try a once-only planet.** Move one ball across just once during the whole activity. If you only watched for a short time, would you know it comes back? This is a **single transit**.

**Accessible version:** Many light-meter apps can show a graph; some light-sensor apps can also turn brightness into sound. Learners can listen for the drop in pitch or volume.

## Discussion questions

1. Why is the dip so small when the planet is tiny?
2. Why did the dip disappear when the path tilted?
3. If you saw a dip only once, what would you need to be sure it was a planet?
4. Could something other than a planet cause a dip? (A cloud, a hand, a flicker of the lamp.)

## Check for understanding (answers for the teacher)

| Question | Expected answer |
|---|---|
| What is a transit? | When a planet passes in front of its star and blocks a little light. |
| Bigger planet → bigger or smaller dip? | Bigger dip. |
| Why can a transit stop happening? | The orbit tilts so the planet no longer passes in front of the star from our point of view. |
| Does the planet disappear? | No. It is still there. We just can't see it pass in front. |

## Extension

- **Make it mathematical (ages 11+):** the fraction of light blocked is about (planet size ÷ star size)². If a planet is 1/10 the width of its star, it blocks about 1/100 of the light (1%). Jupiter is about 1/10 the width of the Sun. Earth is about 1/109 the width of the Sun. How much light would Earth block? (About 1/12,000, or 0.008%.)
- Join the **Planet Hunters TESS** project on Zooniverse, where volunteers look at real TESS light curves.

## For the teacher: the science behind it

**Transit depth.** For a planet of radius *R*ₚ crossing a star of radius *R*★, the fractional dip is approximately δ ≈ (*R*ₚ/*R*★)². Limb darkening (the star's edge looks darker than its center) changes the exact shape.

**Grazing and vanishing transits.** The **impact parameter** *b* is how far from the star's center (in units of the star's radius) the planet crosses. If *b* grows above about 1 + *R*ₚ/*R*★, the planet no longer transits. A second, unseen planet on a tilted orbit can slowly turn ("precess") the first planet's orbit, changing *b* over years.

**TOI-2535 b (🟢 Level 1 — Confirmed observation).** TESS observed TOI-2535 b, a hot Jupiter about 1,000 light-years away, between 2019 and 2026. Its transits became shorter and shallower and were absent in March 2026. Yee et al. (2026, *The Astrophysical Journal Letters*) attribute this to orbital precession caused by an unseen neighbor planet. **How many other such systems are in the archives is an open question (🟡 Level 3).** See [D02](../disclosures/d02-vanishing-transits.md).

**Single transits.** Planets with long orbital periods may cross their star only once during a ~27-day TESS observing sector. These "monotransits" need follow-up to measure the period. See [D03](../disclosures/d03-single-transit-planets.md).

**False positives** include eclipsing binary stars in the background, starspots, instrument effects and spacecraft momentum dumps. Real searches check these carefully.
