# Lesson 1 — Finding What We Cannot See

| | |
|---|---|
| **Ages** | 6–10 |
| **Time** | 30–40 minutes |
| **Group size** | 4–8 learners per sheet |
| **Connects to** | [D01 — Unseen planets in the far Solar System](../disclosures/d01-unseen-planets-far-solar-system.md) |

## Big question

**How can we find something in space that is too dark to see?**

## For young readers

Imagine a big stretchy sheet held tight, like a trampoline. Put a heavy ball on it. The sheet dips down. Now roll a small marble across. The marble curves toward the heavy ball!

Even if you **cover** the heavy ball so you cannot see it, the marble still curves. By watching the marble, you can guess where the hidden ball is.

Space works a bit like this. Heavy things pull on lighter things. Scientists watch how planets and spacecraft move. If they move in a strange way, something unseen might be pulling on them.

## Learning goals

Learners will be able to:

1. Say that heavy objects pull on other objects (gravity).
2. Use the path of a moving object to guess where a hidden object is.
3. Tell the true story of how the planet Neptune was found by its pull before anyone saw it.

## NGSS alignment

- **1-ESS1-1** — patterns that can be predicted (younger learners).
- **5-PS2-1** — gravity pulls objects.
- **MS-ESS1-2** — gravity's role in the Solar System (older learners, extension).

## Materials

- A stretchy fabric (spandex/lycra is best) or a bedsheet, about 1–2 m across
- 1 heavy ball (a softball, a small melon or a 1 kg bag of rice in a sock)
- 5–10 marbles or small balls
- A piece of cardboard about 30 cm × 30 cm (the "cloud" that hides the ball)
- Sticky notes or small paper markers

## Safety

- Hold the sheet at waist height, not above anyone's head.
- Do not throw the heavy ball.
- Pick up marbles from the floor so no one slips.

## Steps

1. **Stretch the sheet.** Four to eight learners hold the edges and pull gently so the sheet is flat.
2. **Roll a marble across the flat sheet.** Ask: "Did it go straight?" (Yes.)
3. **Add the heavy ball** in the middle. Roll the marble again. Ask: "What happened?" (It curved toward the ball or rolled into the dip.)
4. **Hide the ball.** Everyone closes their eyes. The teacher places the heavy ball somewhere on the sheet and holds the cardboard "cloud" just above it, so the ball is hidden but the sheet can still dip.
5. **Be detectives.** Learners open their eyes and take turns rolling marbles from the edge. After each roll, they place a sticky note where the marble's path bent the most.
6. **Make a guess.** When the sticky notes cluster together, learners point to where they think the hidden ball is.
7. **Reveal.** Lift the cardboard. How close was the guess?
8. **Tell the Neptune story** (see "For the teacher"). Astronomers did the same thing with the planet Uranus in the 1840s, and found Neptune!

**Tabletop version (no standing needed):** Stretch a piece of stretchy fabric over a large bowl or embroidery hoop, use a heavy marble or a fishing weight as the hidden mass, and roll small beads.

## Discussion questions

1. Why could you find the ball even when it was hidden?
2. What happens to the marble's path when it passes **far** from the ball? **Close** to it?
3. If the hidden ball were very light, would it be easier or harder to find?
4. Scientists today think there **might** be an unknown planet far beyond Neptune. Why would that be hard to see with a telescope?

## Check for understanding (answers for the teacher)

| Question | Expected answer |
|---|---|
| What made the marble curve? | The dip made by the heavy ball (in space: gravity). |
| How did we find the hidden ball? | By watching how the marbles' paths bent. |
| Which planet was found this way? | Neptune, in 1846. |
| Is this proof that a new planet exists today? | No. It is a way to search. Scientists are still testing the idea. |

## Extension

- Use **two** hidden balls. Is it harder to find both? (Yes. This is like real searches, where many small pulls mix together.)
- Older learners: research "Planet Nine" and list two reasons scientists think it **might** exist and one reason some scientists are not convinced.

## For the teacher: the science behind it

**Model limits.** The stretched sheet is a teaching model. It shows that mass changes the paths of nearby objects, but it is not how gravity really works. On the sheet, the marble is pulled *down* by Earth's gravity into the dip. In space, there is no "down"; masses attract each other directly (Newton), or, in Einstein's description, mass curves spacetime. Say this clearly to older learners so the model does not become a misconception.

**The Neptune story (🟢 Level 1 — Confirmed).** Uranus did not move exactly as predicted from the known planets. In 1845–1846, Urbain Le Verrier (and independently John Couch Adams) calculated where an unseen planet would have to be to cause the difference. Johann Galle found Neptune on 23 September 1846, close to Le Verrier's predicted position.

**Today's search (🟡 Level 3 — Testable idea).** Some distant Kuiper Belt objects have orbits that appear clustered. Batygin & Brown (2016) proposed that an unseen planet of roughly 5–10 Earth masses, a few hundred au from the Sun, could cause this. Other researchers argue that the clustering may come partly from where surveys happened to look (observational bias). No such planet has been found as of November 2026. Precise tracking of spacecraft and pulsar timing can also place limits on where a massive unseen planet could be. See [D01](../disclosures/d01-unseen-planets-far-solar-system.md) for references and the current status.

**Key idea for older learners:** Finding an object from its gravity requires (1) very precise measurements of other objects' motion, (2) a model of **all** known pulls, and (3) careful checks that the leftover differences are not caused by measurement errors.
