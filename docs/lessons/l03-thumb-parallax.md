# Lesson 3 — Thumb Parallax

| | |
|---|---|
| **Ages** | 8–12 |
| **Time** | 30 minutes |
| **Group size** | Pairs |
| **Connects to** | [D04 — Cold, dark neighbors in infrared sky maps](../disclosures/d04-nearby-cold-worlds-wise.md) |

## Big question

**How can we tell how far away a star is, without going there?**

## For young readers

Hold your thumb up at arm's length. Close your left eye. Now open it and close your right eye. Your thumb seems to jump! This jump is called **parallax**.

Now hold your thumb close to your nose and try again. It jumps much more! **Close things jump more. Far things jump less.**

Astronomers use the same trick. Instead of two eyes, they use Earth on two sides of its path around the Sun. Close stars seem to jump a little. Far stars barely move. Some very close, cold, dark objects in space jump a lot — and they also drift across the sky quickly. That makes them easy to miss if a computer is not looking for big jumps.

## Learning goals

Learners will be able to:

1. Show parallax with their thumb.
2. Explain that closer objects show bigger parallax.
3. Explain how Earth's trip around the Sun lets astronomers measure distances to stars.

## NGSS alignment

- **5-ESS1-1** — distance affects how stars appear.
- **MS-ESS1-3** — scale of objects and distances in space.

## Materials

- Your thumbs and eyes
- A ruler or tape measure
- 3 sticky notes (or small pictures) on a far wall
- Paper and pencil

## Safety

- Keep pencils and rulers away from eyes.
- Learners who see with only one eye can do the "two chairs" version (step 7) instead.

## Steps

1. **Put "stars" on the wall.** Place three sticky notes on a wall about 3–5 m away, spaced out.
2. **Thumb at arm's length.** Line up your thumb with the middle sticky note using only your right eye. Now switch eyes. Which sticky note does your thumb now line up with (or near)?
3. **Thumb close.** Hold your thumb halfway to your face. Switch eyes again. Does it jump farther? (Yes.)
4. **Measure.** Your partner measures the distance from your eye to your thumb in both cases. Record how many sticky notes the thumb jumped.
5. **Make a rule.** Together, finish the sentence: "The closer the thumb, the ______ the jump." (bigger)
6. **Connect to space.** Draw the Sun in the middle of a page, Earth on the left side of its path in January and on the right side in July, and a star far above. Draw lines from each Earth position to the star. The angle between the lines is the parallax.
7. **Two chairs version (no switching eyes):** Look at a pencil held by a partner from one chair, then move to a chair 1 m to the side. The pencil seems to move against the far wall. This works the same way and is closer to what astronomers do — they move with Earth.

## Discussion questions

1. Why do astronomers wait six months between pictures?
2. Why is parallax hard to measure for very far stars?
3. A nearby cold object also moves across the sky on its own. If a computer only looks for things that stay in the same spot, would it notice that object?

## Check for understanding (answers for the teacher)

| Question | Expected answer |
|---|---|
| What is parallax? | The way an object seems to shift when you look from two different places. |
| Closer object → bigger or smaller shift? | Bigger shift. |
| What are the "two eyes" for astronomers? | Earth at two sides of its orbit around the Sun, months apart. |

## Extension

- Older learners: a parallax of 1 arcsecond means a distance of 1 **parsec** (about 3.26 light-years). If a star's parallax is ½ arcsecond, how far away is it? (2 parsecs.)
- Join **Backyard Worlds: Planet 9** on Zooniverse. Volunteers look at flip-books of infrared sky images to spot nearby cold objects that move.

## For the teacher: the science behind it

**Stellar parallax (🟢 Level 1).** Friedrich Bessel measured the first stellar parallax, for the star 61 Cygni, in 1838. Distance in parsecs is *d* = 1/ϖ, where ϖ is the parallax angle in arcseconds. Even the nearest star, Proxima Centauri, has a parallax of only about 0.77 arcseconds — about the width of a coin seen from several kilometers away. The European Space Agency's Gaia mission measures parallaxes for over a billion stars.

**Proper motion.** Stars also drift across the sky because they move through space relative to the Sun. Transverse speed (km/s) ≈ 4.74 × μ × *d*, where μ is proper motion in arcseconds per year and *d* is distance in parsecs. Nearby objects can move several arcseconds per year.

**Why this matters for cold neighbors (🟡 Level 3 — Testable idea).** Very cold brown dwarfs and free-floating planets give off almost no visible light but glow in the infrared. NASA's WISE/NEOWISE mission scanned the whole sky many times over more than a decade. A very nearby object would both shift with parallax and drift with proper motion between visits. Matching detections across years requires methods that allow for large motion. Citizen scientists and professionals have already found many cold brown dwarfs this way, including through Backyard Worlds. Whether more remain undiscovered near the Sun is an open question. Previous searches strongly limit how many large objects could still be hiding, so "closer than the nearest star" discoveries would be a long shot. See [D04](../disclosures/d04-nearby-cold-worlds-wise.md).
