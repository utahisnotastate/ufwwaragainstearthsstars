# Lesson 5 — Balance Points in Space

| | |
|---|---|
| **Ages** | 10–14 |
| **Time** | 45 minutes |
| **Group size** | Whole class (walking part) and pairs (drawing part) |
| **Connects to** | [D06 — Dust clouds and possible mini-moons at the Earth–Moon balance points](../disclosures/d06-earth-moon-trojan-points.md) and [D11 — Trojan and co-orbital asteroids near Venus, Earth and Mars](../disclosures/d11-inner-solar-system-trojans.md) |

## Big question

**Are there places in space where small objects can travel along with a planet?**

## For young readers

When a planet goes around the Sun, there are special spots that move along with it. Two of these spots are a little ahead of the planet and a little behind it. A small rock in one of these spots can follow the planet for a very long time, like a puppy trotting beside its owner.

These spots are called **Lagrange points**. The rocks that live near them are called **Trojans**.

Jupiter has thousands of Trojan asteroids. A NASA spacecraft called **Lucy** is on its way to visit some of them. Earth has a few small Trojans too. Scientists think more might be hiding in the twilight sky, where they are hard to see.

## Learning goals

Learners will be able to:

1. Find the L4 and L5 points by drawing an equilateral triangle.
2. Explain that Trojans share a planet's orbit, about 60° ahead or behind.
3. Explain why objects near the Sun in our sky are hard to find (twilight glare).

## NGSS alignment

- **MS-ESS1-2** — gravity and motion in the Solar System.
- **MS-PS2-4** — gravitational attraction depends on mass.

## Materials

- Chalk (outdoors) or masking tape (indoors) and a 4–6 m piece of string
- Paper, a drawing compass, a ruler, a protractor, colored pencils
- Optional: a photo of the Lucy spacecraft from NASA

## Safety

- Walk slowly in the walking activity. Keep a clear circle.
- Compass points are sharp; younger learners can trace around a jar lid instead.

## Steps

### Part A — Draw the balance points

1. Draw a dot in the middle of the page and label it **Sun**.
2. Draw a big circle around it (the planet's orbit). Put a dot on the circle: **Planet**.
3. Set your compass to the distance from the Sun to the Planet.
4. Put the compass point on the **Sun** and draw a small arc that crosses the orbit. Do the same from the **Planet**. Where the arcs cross the orbit (one ahead, one behind the planet) are **L4** and **L5**.
5. Connect Sun, Planet and L4. You made an **equilateral triangle** — all sides equal, all angles 60°. Check with your protractor.

### Part B — Walk the orbit

6. Make a big circle with chalk or tape (use the string from a center point). One learner stands in the middle as the **Sun**.
7. One learner is the **Planet** and walks slowly around the circle.
8. Two learners are **Trojans**. One stays 60° ahead of the Planet, one stays 60° behind, both on the circle. (Hint: they should stay the same distance from the Planet as the Planet is from the Sun.)
9. Everyone walks together. Notice: the Trojans never catch up with the Planet and never fall far behind.

### Part C — The twilight problem

10. Ask: "If you stand on Earth at night, which way do you face — toward the Sun or away?" (Away.) Earth's Trojans sit about 60° from the Sun in our sky. They are only visible low in the sky around sunset or sunrise, when the sky is bright. That is why they are hard to find.

**Accessible version:** Do Part A with tactile graphics (pins in cork board joined with yarn), and Part B as a tabletop activity with three toy figures on a lazy Susan turntable marked at 60° spacing.

## Discussion questions

1. Why do Trojans not crash into the planet?
2. Why are Jupiter's Trojans easier to find than Earth's?
3. The Earth and Moon also have L4 and L5 points. Faint dust clouds may live there. Why is it hard to be sure?

## Check for understanding (answers for the teacher)

| Question | Expected answer |
|---|---|
| Where are L4 and L5? | About 60° ahead of and behind the planet, on its orbit. |
| What shape do the Sun, planet and L4 make? | An equilateral triangle. |
| What are Trojans? | Small bodies sharing a planet's orbit near L4 or L5. |
| Why are Earth Trojans hard to see? | They appear in the twilight sky, near the Sun's direction, where glare hides faint objects. |

## Extension

- There are five Lagrange points. Research L1, L2 and L3. Which one is the James Webb Space Telescope near? (It orbits around the Sun–Earth L2 point.)
- Older learners: the triangular points are stable only if the big body is at least about 25 times heavier than the smaller one. The Sun is about 1,000 times heavier than Jupiter. The Earth is about 81 times heavier than the Moon. Both pass this test, but the Earth–Moon points are also tugged by the Sun.

## For the teacher: the science behind it

**Lagrange points (🟢 Level 1).** In the circular restricted three-body problem, there are five equilibrium points in the rotating frame. L4 and L5 form equilateral triangles with the two large bodies. They are linearly stable when the mass ratio satisfies *M*₁/*M*₂ ≳ 25 (Routh's criterion, about 24.96). Objects near L4/L5 move on "tadpole" orbits; some co-orbitals move on larger "horseshoe" orbits.

**Known Trojans (🟢 Level 1).** Jupiter has more than ten thousand known Trojans; NASA's Lucy mission, launched in October 2021, will fly past several of them. Mars has a small number, including (5261) Eureka. Earth's first known Trojan, 2010 TK7, was found in data from NASA's WISE infrared telescope (Connors et al. 2011). A second, 2020 XL5, was confirmed in 2021 (Santana-Ros et al. 2022).

**Earth–Moon L4/L5 (🔵 Level 2 for dust, 🟠 Level 4 for solid bodies).** Kazimierz Kordylewski reported faint clouds at the Earth–Moon L5 point in 1961. Imaging polarimetry by Slíz-Balogh et al. (2019) reported evidence supporting the dust clouds, but the result is still discussed. Because the Sun strongly perturbs the Earth–Moon triangular points, long-term stable solid bodies there are considered unlikely, and searches have not found any. See [D06](../disclosures/d06-earth-moon-trojan-points.md).

**Inner Solar System co-orbitals (🟡 Level 3).** Objects sharing orbits with Venus and Earth spend much of their time at small angles from the Sun, where ground surveys observe only in twilight. Dedicated twilight surveys have started to find objects inside Earth's orbit. How many co-orbitals remain undiscovered is an open question. See [D11](../disclosures/d11-inner-solar-system-trojans.md).
