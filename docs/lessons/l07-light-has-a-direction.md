# Lesson 7 — Light Has a Direction

| | |
|---|---|
| **Ages** | 10–14 |
| **Time** | 40 minutes |

## Big question

**Can the way light "wiggles" help us tell a planet's light apart from its star's light?**

## For young readers

Light is a wave. It wiggles as it travels. Light from a lamp or a star wiggles in every direction at once, all mixed up.

But when light bounces off something — like a lake, a window or a planet's clouds — it can start to wiggle mostly in **one** direction. This is called **polarized** light.

Polarized sunglasses block light that wiggles one way. That is how they cut glare from water. You can use them to explore polarized light today!

Scientists hope polarization can help them spot planets next to bright stars. It is very, very hard, and it needs giant telescopes. But the idea is real.

## Learning goals

Learners will be able to:

1. Show that some light is polarized, using polarized sunglasses.
2. Explain that reflected light is often polarized.
3. Explain why polarization could help separate a planet's light from its star's light, and why it is hard.

## NGSS alignment

- **MS-PS4-2** — light is reflected, absorbed or transmitted by materials.
- **4-PS4-2** — reflected light lets us see objects.

## Materials

- Polarized sunglasses (check the label) or two pieces of polarizing film
- A phone, tablet or computer screen (most LCD screens give off polarized light)
- A shiny tabletop, a window, or a tray of water
- Clear plastic tape and a clear plastic sheet or bag (for the extension)

## Safety

- **Never look at the Sun**, even with sunglasses or polarizing film.
- Do not leave phones or screens near the water tray.

## Steps

1. **Screen test.** Turn on a phone or computer screen with a white background. Look at it through one lens of the sunglasses. Slowly rotate the sunglasses (or the phone). What happens? (On most screens, it gets dark at one angle.) The screen's light is polarized.
2. **Two-lens test.** Hold two polarizing films (or two pairs of polarized sunglasses) in front of each other and rotate one. At one angle they go almost black. Each film only lets one direction of wiggle through.
3. **Glare test.** Look at the glare on a shiny table or tray of water from a low angle. Look through the sunglasses and tilt your head. The glare almost vanishes at one angle. Reflected light is polarized!
4. **Sky test (outdoors, with an adult).** With your back to the Sun, look at blue sky through the sunglasses and rotate them. Parts of the sky get darker and lighter. Sunlight scattered by air is partly polarized.
5. **Connect to planets.** Light from a star is almost unpolarized (mixed). Light reflected by a planet's clouds or surface is partly polarized. In principle, a polarizing filter can help pick out the planet's light.
6. **The hard part.** Discuss: the planet is millions to billions of times fainter than its star. The polarized part is even smaller. That is why this needs very large telescopes and very careful instruments.

**Accessible version:** Use a light-sensor app with sound output. Place the sensor behind the sunglasses in front of the screen and rotate; the tone changes as the light dims.

## Discussion questions

1. Why do fishers and drivers like polarized sunglasses?
2. Why is star light mostly unpolarized?
3. Why would a planet's light be partly polarized?
4. A backyard telescope collects much less light than a giant telescope. Why does that matter for this method?

## Check for understanding (answers for the teacher)

| Question | Expected answer |
|---|---|
| What is polarized light? | Light whose waves wiggle mostly in one direction. |
| What makes light polarized? | Reflection and scattering (also some filters and screens). |
| Why might polarization help find planets? | Starlight is mostly unpolarized; reflected planet light is partly polarized. |
| Why is it hard? | The planet's polarized light is a tiny fraction of the total; it needs huge telescopes and very precise instruments. |

## Extension

- **Stress colors:** Put clear plastic (a fork, a ruler, or layers of tape on a plastic sheet) between two crossed polarizers. Colors appear where the plastic is stressed. Engineers use this to check for stress in materials.
- Older learners: research the instrument SPHERE on the European Southern Observatory's Very Large Telescope, which uses polarimetry to image dusty disks around young stars.

## For the teacher: the science behind it

**Polarization by reflection and scattering (🟢 Level 1).** Light reflected from a dielectric surface near Brewster's angle is strongly linearly polarized. Rayleigh scattering in the sky gives maximum polarization about 90° from the Sun. LCD screens use polarizers, so their light is linearly polarized.

**Planets and polarimetry (🟢 Level 1 for the physics; 🟠 Level 4 for small telescopes).** Thermal light from a Sun-like star is nearly unpolarized when averaged over the disk. Starlight reflected by a planet's atmosphere can be substantially polarized at intermediate phase angles. However, the planet-to-star flux ratio is tiny — of order 10⁻⁵ to 10⁻⁴ for the most favorable hot Jupiters in reflected light and of order 10⁻⁹ for a Jupiter-like planet at Jupiter-like distances. The **net** polarization of the unresolved star-plus-planet system is therefore expected to be of order 10⁻⁵ or smaller. Claimed detections at the 10⁻⁴ level for HD 189733 b were not confirmed by other instruments. High-contrast imaging polarimeters on 8-m telescopes routinely image dusty disks, and polarimetry is a promising method for future large telescopes. A consumer telescope with a beam splitter cannot reach the required precision because photon noise alone limits the precision to roughly 1/√N, where N is the number of detected photons. See [D05](../disclosures/d05-polarized-light-from-planets.md).
