# Lesson 8 — Radio Auroras

| | |
|---|---|
| **Ages** | 10–14 |
| **Time** | 45 minutes |
| **Group size** | Small groups |
| **Connects to** | [D08 — Radio auroras from planets and brown dwarfs](../disclosures/d08-radio-auroras.md) |

## Big question

**Can we "hear" planets and failed stars that we cannot see?**

## For young readers

Have you seen pictures of the **northern lights**? Those glowing curtains in the sky are called **auroras**. They happen when tiny charged bits from the Sun are pulled by Earth's magnetic field toward the north and south poles.

Auroras do not only glow. They also send out **radio waves**. Our eyes cannot see radio waves, but radio antennas can pick them up.

Jupiter has huge auroras and is a loud radio source. Some **brown dwarfs** (objects bigger than planets but too small to shine like stars) have auroras too! In 2020, scientists found a brand-new brown dwarf by "hearing" its radio signal first. Some objects might be too dim to see with normal telescopes, but loud enough in radio.

## Learning goals

Learners will be able to:

1. Show a magnetic field's shape with iron filings.
2. Explain that auroras happen where charged particles follow magnetic fields.
3. Explain that auroras give off radio waves that radio telescopes can detect.

## Materials

- A bar magnet
- Iron filings **sealed** inside a clear plastic bottle or a zip bag taped shut (or a store-bought magnetic field viewer)
- A small compass
- A ball or globe (to stand for a planet)
- Optional: an AM radio (to hear natural radio crackle from distant lightning), and pictures of Jupiter's auroras from the Hubble Space Telescope or Juno mission

## Safety

- Keep iron filings sealed. They can irritate eyes and skin.
- Keep magnets away from phones, bank cards, pacemakers and young children.

## Steps

1. **See the invisible.** Lay the sealed filings over the bar magnet. Tap gently. Lines appear, curving from one end of the magnet to the other. These show the **magnetic field**.
2. **Find the poles.** Where do the lines bunch together? (At the ends — the poles.)
3. **Compass check.** Move the compass around the magnet. The needle follows the lines.
4. **Model a planet.** Tape the magnet inside or behind a ball. Explain: Earth acts like it has a giant magnet inside.
5. **Make an aurora (pretend).** Learners use small pieces of paper as "charged particles from the Sun" and slide them along the field lines toward the poles. Where they "land" near the poles is where auroras glow.
6. **Listen.** Optional: tune an AM radio between stations. The crackles you might hear are often from lightning far away — natural radio waves! Explain that auroras on Earth and Jupiter also make radio waves, at frequencies that need special radios or spacecraft to hear.
7. **Big idea.** A cold brown dwarf can be very dark in visible light but still have strong auroras. A radio telescope might detect it even when an ordinary telescope cannot see it.

**Accessible version:** Learners can feel the magnet's pull on a paper clip at different places, and the teacher can describe the filing patterns. Many space agencies publish audio versions ("sonifications") of real radio data.

## Discussion questions

1. Why do auroras appear near the poles?
2. Jupiter's magnetic field is much stronger than Earth's. Would you expect stronger or weaker radio auroras? (Stronger.)
3. Why might radio help find objects that are too dark to see?
4. Radio telescopes also pick up radio noise from phones, TV and satellites. How might scientists tell a real space signal from human-made noise?

## Check for understanding (answers for the teacher)

| Question | Expected answer |
|---|---|
| What causes auroras? | Charged particles guided by a magnetic field into the upper atmosphere near the poles. |
| What else do auroras give off besides light? | Radio waves. |
| Name an object besides Earth with radio auroras. | Jupiter (also Saturn, and some brown dwarfs). |
| Why is radio useful for dark objects? | They may be bright in radio even if they are dim in visible light. |

## Extension

- Look up **Radio JOVE**, a NASA-supported project where students and amateurs build simple radio telescopes to listen to Jupiter and the Sun.
- Older learners: the radio frequency from auroras depends on magnetic field strength. Stronger fields give higher frequencies.

## For the teacher: the science behind it

**Auroral radio emission (🟢 Level 1).** Magnetized planets emit intense, circularly polarized radio emission from their auroral regions through the **electron cyclotron maser instability**. The emission frequency is close to the local electron cyclotron frequency, *f*꜀ ≈ 2.8 MHz × (*B*/1 gauss). Earth's auroral kilometric radiation (roughly 50–500 kHz) cannot reach the ground because the ionosphere blocks it, so it was discovered by spacecraft. Jupiter's decametric emission (up to about 40 MHz) can reach the ground; it was discovered in 1955 by Burke and Franklin and is observed by amateurs.

**Brown dwarfs (🟢 Level 1).** Hallinan et al. (2015) showed that the ultracool dwarf LSR J1835+3259 has auroral emission, including optical signatures. In 2020, astronomers searched the LOFAR Two-metre Sky Survey for circularly polarized radio sources and found a previously unknown cold brown dwarf (BDR J1750+3809, about 65 parsecs away), confirmed afterwards with infrared observations (Vedantham et al. 2020). This was the first brown dwarf discovered by its radio emission, and it shows that low-frequency radio surveys can find substellar objects.

**The open question (🟡 Level 3).** Can archival low-frequency radio data (for example from LOFAR surveys) reveal auroral emission from brown dwarfs or exoplanets that are not yet known? Tests include circular polarization, time variability and matching with infrared catalogs. Human-made radio interference is the main false positive. See [D08](../disclosures/d08-radio-auroras.md).
