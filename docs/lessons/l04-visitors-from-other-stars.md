# Lesson 4 — Visitors from Other Stars

| | |
|---|---|
| **Ages** | 9–13 |
| **Time** | 45–60 minutes |
| **Group size** | 3–4 learners per station |
| **Connects to** | [D14 — Interstellar visitors in unlinked asteroid data](../disclosures/d14-interstellar-objects-unlinked-data.md) |

## Big question

**How can we tell if a rock in space came from another star?**

## For young readers

Most space rocks in our Solar System go around and around the Sun. Their paths are loops shaped like stretched circles. These loops are called **ellipses**.

But a few rocks are just passing through! They come from far away, swing past the Sun once, and leave forever. Their paths are open curves, like a wide letter "U" with the ends spread out. This shape is called a **hyperbola**.

The trick is **speed**. Slow rocks get caught by the Sun's gravity and loop around. Rocks that are fast enough escape.

Scientists have found three visitors from other stars so far: **ʻOumuamua** (2017), **Borisov** (2019) and **3I/ATLAS** (2025). There are probably more out there!

## Learning goals

Learners will be able to:

1. Tell the difference between a closed orbit (ellipse) and an open path (hyperbola).
2. Explain that speed decides whether an object is caught by the Sun or escapes.
3. Name the three known interstellar objects.

## NGSS alignment

- **MS-ESS1-2** — gravity controls motion in the Solar System.
- **HS-ESS1-4** — math models of orbits (extension for older learners).

## Materials

**Station A — Gravity funnel**
- A large funnel, a big mixing bowl, or a "gravity well" coin funnel (from a science shop or museum)
- Marbles or coins

**Station B — Drawing orbits**
- Cardboard (at least 40 cm × 40 cm), paper on top
- 2 push pins (or tape for younger learners)
- A loop of string about 25 cm around
- For the hyperbola (older learners): a ruler about 30 cm long, a third pin, and a piece of string about 20 cm long

## Safety

- Push pins are sharp. Adults can place pins for younger learners, or use tape.
- Pick up marbles from the floor.

## Steps

### Station A — Caught or escaping?

1. Roll a marble **slowly** along the inside edge of the bowl. It circles around and around. **Caught!** That is like an orbit.
2. Flick a marble **fast** across the bowl so it swoops near the middle and out the other side. It curves once and leaves. **Escaped!** That is like an interstellar visitor.
3. Try different speeds. Find the speed where the marble just barely escapes.

### Station B — Draw the shapes

4. **Ellipse:** Put two pins in the paper about 8 cm apart. Place the string loop around both pins. Pull the loop tight with a pencil and draw all the way around. You made an ellipse! The Sun sits at one pin.
5. Move the pins closer. Does the ellipse look more like a circle? (Yes.)
6. **Hyperbola (ages 11+):** Pin one end of the ruler at point F1 so it can swing. Tie the string from the free end of the ruler to a second pin at F2. Hold the string tight against the ruler with your pencil and swing the ruler. The pencil draws one open curve — a hyperbola.
7. Label your drawings: "closed orbit — stays forever" and "open path — visits once."

### Whole group

8. Share the table below. Which visitor was fastest?

| Visitor | Found | Found by | Speed far from the Sun (approx.) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1I/ʻOumuamua | October 2017 | Pan-STARRS survey telescope, Hawaiʻi | about 26 km/s (about 94,000 km/h) |
| 2I/Borisov | August 2019 | Gennady Borisov, an amateur astronomer, with a telescope he built | about 32 km/s (about 115,000 km/h) |
| 3I/ATLAS | July 2025 | ATLAS survey telescope, Chile | about 58 km/s (about 209,000 km/h) |

## Discussion questions

1. Why do most asteroids stay in loops?
2. What makes an interstellar visitor different?
3. 2I/Borisov was discovered by one person with a homemade telescope. What does that tell you about who can make discoveries?
4. Why might a computer that expects loop-shaped orbits have trouble with a fast visitor?

## Check for understanding (answers for the teacher)

| Question | Expected answer |
|---|---|
| Shape of a normal orbit? | Ellipse (closed loop). |
| Shape of an interstellar visitor's path? | Hyperbola (open curve). |
| What decides which one? | Speed (compared with the escape speed at that distance). |
| Name one interstellar object. | ʻOumuamua, Borisov or 3I/ATLAS. |

## Extension

- At Earth's distance from the Sun, the **escape speed** is about 42 km/s. A rock passing Earth's distance faster than that cannot stay. Is 3I/ATLAS's speed far from the Sun faster than 42 km/s? (Yes, about 58 km/s, and it moves even faster near the Sun.)
- Older learners: try [Lesson 12](l12-high-school-hyperbolic-orbits.md) to calculate these speeds yourself.

## For the teacher: the science behind it

**Conic sections (🟢 Level 1).** Under the Sun's gravity alone, a body follows a conic section with the Sun at one focus. Eccentricity *e* sets the shape: 0 ≤ *e* < 1 ellipse (bound), *e* = 1 parabola, *e* > 1 hyperbola (unbound). The specific orbital energy ε = *v*²/2 − *GM*/*r* is negative for bound orbits and positive for unbound orbits. Far from the Sun, an unbound body keeps the "hyperbolic excess speed" *v*∞ = √(2ε).

**The funnel model.** A funnel shows the idea of being "caught" vs "escaping", but friction makes marbles spiral inward, which real orbits do not. Point this out.

**The hyperbola construction.** For a point P on the pencil, |PF1 − PF2| equals the ruler length minus the string length, which is constant — the defining property of a hyperbola.

**Known interstellar objects (🟢 Level 1).** Approximate orbital values (check the JPL Small-Body Database for current solutions): 1I/ʻOumuamua *e* ≈ 1.20, *v*∞ ≈ 26 km/s; 2I/Borisov *e* ≈ 3.36, *v*∞ ≈ 32 km/s; 3I/ATLAS *e* ≈ 6.1, *v*∞ ≈ 58 km/s (Seligman et al. 2025).

**The open question (🟡 Level 3).** Whether undetected interstellar objects are among unlinked asteroid observations held by the Minor Planet Center is not known. It can be tested by trying to link those observations with orbit models that allow unbound paths. See [D14](../disclosures/d14-interstellar-objects-unlinked-data.md).
