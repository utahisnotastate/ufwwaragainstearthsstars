# Guide for Students and Future Space Scientists

> **In one sentence:** If you dream of working at NASA, ESA, an observatory or a space company, this page shows the skills to build and the real programs that can help — and how this project can be your practice ground.

## For young readers

Do you want to work in space science one day? Great! Here is the secret: real scientists started by being curious, practicing math, and asking lots of questions. You can start now. Try the [lessons](../lessons/README.md), join a citizen-science project, and keep a notebook of your questions.

## There is more than one path

People who work on space missions and astronomy come from many fields:

| Field | What they do | Example tasks in this project |
|---|---|---|
| **Astronomy and astrophysics** | Study stars, planets and galaxies | Testing whether a disclosure's idea holds up |
| **Planetary science** | Study planets, moons, asteroids, comets | Sorting orbits with the Tisserand parameter ([D15](../disclosures/d15-captured-interstellar-objects.md)) |
| **Physics** | Understand the laws behind everything | Orbit mechanics and statistics ([guide for physicists](physicists.md)) |
| **Computer science and data science** | Build software, handle huge data sets | Improving the `fdastro` code and tests |
| **Engineering** (aerospace, electrical, mechanical, software, systems) | Design spacecraft, instruments, ground systems | Understanding how instruments limit what we can detect |
| **Mathematics and statistics** | Models, algorithms, uncertainty | Measuring false-alarm rates |
| **Science communication and education** | Explain science to the public | Writing lessons, translating pages |
| **Operations, policy, law, management** | Keep missions running and funded | Planning and project management |

You do not need to be "the best at math" to work in space. You need to be **persistent**, **careful** and **curious**.

## Skills to build (by stage)

### Ages 10–14

- **Math:** fractions, ratios, graphs, and the idea of scale.
- **Science habits:** make a prediction, test it, write down what happened.
- **Reading and writing:** explain an idea in your own words.
- **Try:** the [lessons](../lessons/README.md), astronomy clubs, planetarium visits, and safe stargazing with an adult.

### Ages 14–18

- **Math:** algebra, trigonometry, then calculus if you can.
- **Physics:** motion, forces, energy, gravity, waves and light.
- **Programming:** Python is widely used in astronomy. Learn variables, loops, functions, plotting, and reading data files.
- **Statistics:** averages, spread, and what "error bars" mean.
- **Try:** [Lesson 12](../lessons/l12-high-school-hyperbolic-orbits.md) and [Tutorial 2](../tutorials/t02-orbits-ellipses-and-hyperbolas.md).

### University

- **Core:** classical mechanics, electromagnetism, quantum and statistical physics, linear algebra, differential equations, numerical methods, probability and statistics.
- **Tools:** Python (NumPy, SciPy, Matplotlib, Astropy), version control (Git), the command line, writing in LaTeX.
- **Research habits:** reading papers on arXiv and NASA ADS, reproducing a result, writing clear reports.
- **Try:** [Lesson 13](../lessons/l13-university-lab-orbit-fitting.md) and the full set of [tutorials](../tutorials/).

## Real programs you can join

Program details change every year. Always check the official website for current eligibility and deadlines.

### Citizen science (any age with adult help; no degree needed)

| Program | What you do | Where |
|---|---|---|
| **Backyard Worlds: Planet 9** | Spot moving cold brown dwarfs and possible distant planets in infrared sky images | Zooniverse |
| **Planet Hunters TESS** | Look for transit dips in TESS light curves | Zooniverse |
| **International Astronomical Search Collaboration (IASC)** | High school and college teams search new telescope images for unknown asteroids | IASC website (school-based) |
| **Exoplanet Watch** | Observe or analyze exoplanet transits to refine their timing; led by NASA JPL | NASA science website |

Volunteers in these projects have helped discover real brown dwarfs, planets and asteroids, and some have been named as co-authors on scientific papers.

### Internships and early-career programs (United States)

| Program | Who it is for | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **NASA OSTEM Internships** | High school (usually 16+) and college students | Paid. Apply at intern.nasa.gov. Eligibility (including U.S. citizenship and GPA) applies. |
| **NASA Pathways Program** | College students | Can lead to full-time NASA employment. |
| **National Space Grant College and Fellowship Program** | College students | NASA-funded consortia in every U.S. state offer scholarships, internships and research. |
| **NSF Research Experiences for Undergraduates (REU)** | College students | Paid summer research at universities and observatories, many in astronomy and physics. |

### Outside the United States

Many space agencies run student programs. Examples include the **European Space Agency (ESA)** traineeships and Young Graduate Trainee program, and university summer research programs worldwide. Search your national space agency's education pages.

## How this project can help you

- **Practice real methods.** Our tutorials use the same kinds of methods as professional research: orbit fitting, light-curve analysis, parallax, and statistics.
- **Build a portfolio.** A clear analysis notebook, a bug fix, or a well-written lesson on GitHub is evidence of your skills.
- **Learn scientific honesty.** Every disclosure separates what is confirmed from what is a guess. Employers and graduate schools value people who report uncertainty honestly.

### Good first contributions

1. Run a tutorial and report anything confusing (open an issue).
2. Add a test to the `fdastro` package.
3. Translate a lesson into another language.
4. Re-check a number in a disclosure against its original paper and report what you find.

See [CONTRIBUTING.md](../../CONTRIBUTING.md) for how to start.

## A word about discoveries

Discoveries are exciting, but most "discoveries" in data turn out to be noise, known objects, or instrument effects. That is normal. Learning to check carefully is the most valuable skill you can build. If you think you have found something, read [how to report a discovery](../about/how-to-report-a-discovery.md) before announcing it.
