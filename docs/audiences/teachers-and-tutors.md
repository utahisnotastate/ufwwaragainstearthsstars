# Guide for Teachers and Tutors

> **In one sentence:** Ready-to-use lessons, from age 6 to university, that teach how astronomers detect things they cannot see — with honest evidence labels you can use to teach scientific thinking.

## For young readers

This page is for teachers and tutors. If you are a student, ask your teacher about the [lessons](../lessons/README.md)!

## Why use this project in your teaching

1. **Real, current science.** Lessons connect to discoveries from 2017–2026, including the three known interstellar objects and the vanishing transits of TOI-2535 b.
2. **Evidence literacy built in.** Every claim has an evidence level (Confirmed, Strong evidence, Testable idea, Long shot). Students learn to ask "How do we know?" and "How sure are we?"
3. **Low-cost materials.** Most lessons use lamps, balls, string, sunglasses and a playground.
4. **A path from wonder to research.** The same idea appears as a hands-on lesson for children, a calculation for high school, and a coding lab for university students.
5. **Free and open.** Text is licensed CC BY 4.0 and code is MIT-licensed. You may adapt, translate and share with attribution.

## How the lessons are built

Each lesson contains:

- Age band and time
- A **big question**
- Learning goals
- NGSS alignment (performance expectations the lesson **supports**)
- Materials and **safety**
- Numbered steps, plus an **accessible version**
- Discussion questions
- A check for understanding **with answers**
- An extension for faster learners
- **"For the teacher: the science behind it"** — the expert layer with equations, evidence labels and references
- A link to the matching disclosure

See the full [lesson index](../lessons/README.md).

## Common misconceptions (and how the lessons address them)

| Misconception | Better idea | Where addressed |
|---|---|---|
| "Planets orbit in perfect circles." | Orbits are ellipses; some paths are open hyperbolas. | L4, L12 |
| "There is no gravity in space." | Gravity acts everywhere; astronauts float because they are falling around Earth. | L1, L5 |
| "Light arrives instantly." | Light takes time: 8 minutes from the Sun, over 4 years from the nearest other star. | L9 |
| "A transit is the planet blocking the whole star." | A transit blocks a tiny fraction (often less than 1%) of the star's light. | L2 |
| "If a transit stops, the planet is gone." | The planet can still be there on a tilted orbit. | L2 |
| "Moons orbit the center of the planet." | Planet and moon both orbit their shared barycenter. | L11 |
| "Alien planets means aliens." | It means planets around other stars; no aliens have been found. | Kids page, FAQ |
| "Scientists hide discoveries." | Unexplored data reflects limited time and resources, not secrecy. | FAQ, all disclosures |
| "A backwards orbit proves an object came from another star." | It is a clue, not proof; there are local explanations too. | L6 |
| "Gravity is like a trampoline." | The trampoline is a model with limits; on it, Earth's gravity does the pulling. | L1 (teacher notes) |

## Differentiation

**For younger or emerging readers**
- Read the "For young readers" box aloud.
- Use the tabletop or accessible version of each activity.
- Replace written responses with drawings or verbal explanations.

**For English language learners**
- Pre-teach 3–5 words from the [glossary](../about/glossary.md) (each has a kid-friendly line).
- Use the diagrams and physical models as shared reference points.
- Pair students for discussion questions.

**For advanced learners**
- Assign the extension and the "For the teacher" section as reading.
- Connect to the matching [disclosure](../disclosures/README.md) and ask students to propose a test using public data.
- Older students can attempt the [tutorials](../tutorials/).

**For learners with disabilities**
- Each lesson has an accessible version. Light-sensor apps with sound output make brightness changes audible.
- Wheelchair users can take central roles in walking activities, or use tabletop versions.

## Assessment ideas

1. **Claim–Evidence–Reasoning (CER).** Prompt: "Is 3I/ATLAS from another star?" Students state a claim, cite evidence (speed, eccentricity), and explain the reasoning (energy, escape speed).
2. **Evidence-level sorting.** Give students 8 statements from the disclosures. They label each Level 1–4 and justify.
3. **Model critique.** "What does the trampoline model get right? What does it get wrong?"
4. **Data graph.** Students draw and interpret a light curve (L2) or a timing–duration loop (L11).
5. **Exit ticket.** "One thing astronomers can find without seeing it directly, and how."

### Sample rubric: Claim–Evidence–Reasoning

| | 3 — Proficient | 2 — Developing | 1 — Beginning |
|---|---|---|---|
| Claim | Clear, answers the question | Partly answers | Missing or off-topic |
| Evidence | Specific data, correct units | Some data | No data |
| Reasoning | Connects evidence to a physical principle | Connection is vague | No connection |
| Uncertainty | Notes limits or evidence level | Mentions doubt vaguely | None |

## Safety summary

- **Never look at the Sun**, with or without sunglasses, binoculars, telescopes or phones.
- Use LED lamps; incandescent bulbs get hot.
- Adults handle glass; use plastic where possible.
- Seal iron filings; keep strong magnets away from small children, phones, cards and pacemakers.
- Walking activities: walk, keep lanes clear, stop if anyone is dizzy.
- No laser pointers are needed.

## Teaching about uncertainty and controversy

Several topics are open research questions (for example, exomoon candidates and the Planet Nine hypothesis). Present them honestly:

- Describe what is known, what is claimed, and what the critics say.
- Use the evidence labels.
- Emphasize that disagreement is normal and healthy in science; it is how errors get caught.

## Tutoring tips (one-to-one)

- Start with the student's question, then find the lesson or disclosure that connects.
- Use "predict → test → explain": ask for a prediction before every activity step.
- Keep a "science notebook" where the student records predictions, data and surprises.
- For older students, pair one lesson with the matching tutorial to bridge hands-on and computational thinking.
