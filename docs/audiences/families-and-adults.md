# Guide for Families and Curious Adults

> **In one sentence:** This project explains, in plain language, where new discoveries about planets and space rocks may be waiting in public science data — and how sure scientists are about each idea.

## For young readers

This page is for grown-ups. If you are a kid, try the [kids' page](kids.md)!

## What this project is

Modern telescopes and spacecraft collect more data than anyone can study in detail. Much of it is freely available to the public. **Future Disclosure: Astronomy & Alien Planets** is an independent, open-source project that:

1. **Lists 15 "disclosures"** — specific places in public data where discoveries could be found. See the [disclosure registry](../disclosures/README.md).
2. **Labels each claim** with an evidence level, from "confirmed" to "long shot".
3. **Shows how anyone can test** each idea, with free software and step-by-step tutorials.
4. **Teaches the methods** with hands-on lessons for children and students.

"Alien planets" here means **exoplanets** — planets around other stars. We make no claims about alien life or alien technology.

## What this project is not

- **Not a conspiracy.** Nobody is hiding planets. Scientists leave some signals unexplored because of limited time, limited telescope access, and the high cost of false alarms. Our goal is to help, not to blame.
- **Not affiliated with NASA or any agency.** We use public data from NASA, ESA and others, but they do not endorse this project.
- **Not health advice.** Earlier brainstorming notes included health and "frequency" claims. Those were removed because there is no good evidence for them. See the [editorial notes](../about/editorial-notes.md).

## How to read our evidence labels

| Label | What it means for you |
|---|---|
| 🟢 **Level 1 — Confirmed** | You can state this as fact. It is published and independently checked. |
| 🔵 **Level 2 — Strong evidence** | Real published results exist, but experts are still debating them. Say "scientists have strong evidence that…" |
| 🟡 **Level 3 — Testable idea** | Reasonable and testable, but not shown yet. Say "some scientists think it is worth checking whether…" |
| 🟠 **Level 4 — Long shot** | The physics allows it, but current tools may be too weak. Say "it is possible, but unlikely to be found soon." |

## How to spot space hype online

Space news attracts exaggeration. Here are quick checks you can use with your family:

1. **Is there a paper?** Real discoveries are usually described in a scientific paper. News that cites only "a source" or a video is weaker.
2. **Who checked it?** One team's result is exciting. Two independent teams agreeing is much stronger.
3. **Watch for absolute words.** "Proves", "confirmed alien", "NASA is hiding" are red flags.
4. **Look for the uncertainty.** Real measurements come with error bars. "About 1,000 km across, give or take 400" is more trustworthy than "exactly 1,000 km".
5. **Check the date.** Space science moves fast. A 2015 article may be out of date.

## The most exciting real stories (as of November 2026)

- **Three interstellar visitors.** ʻOumuamua (2017), Borisov (2019) and 3I/ATLAS (2025) passed through the Solar System on open, one-time paths. Radio searches pointed at them found no signs of technology; all three are best explained as natural objects. 🟢
- **A vanishing planet.** TESS watched the transits of the giant planet TOI-2535 b shrink and disappear between 2019 and 2026. Astronomers think an unseen neighboring planet is slowly tilting its orbit (Yee et al. 2026). 🟢
- **A new distant world.** The Vera C. Rubin Observatory reported 2026 ED138, a large icy object beyond Neptune and a dwarf-planet candidate. 🔵 (candidate status)
- **Lonely planets.** Microlensing surveys have found planet-mass objects that seem to drift between the stars without a parent star. 🔵

## Ways your family can take part

**No equipment needed:**
- Do the [hands-on lessons](../lessons/README.md) at the kitchen table.
- Join citizen science projects on Zooniverse, such as **Backyard Worlds: Planet 9** and **Planet Hunters TESS**. Volunteers there have contributed to real discoveries.

**With a computer:**
- Follow the [tutorials](../tutorials/) to run the same kinds of analyses researchers use, on real public data.

**With a telescope:**
- See the [guide for amateur astronomers](amateur-astronomers.md). Amateurs contribute real measurements of asteroids, comets, star occultations and exoplanet transits.

## Talking with children about space

- **"I don't know" is a great answer.** Follow it with "How could we find out?"
- **Let them guess first,** then test. That is the scientific method.
- **Praise careful checking,** not just big claims.
- **Keep it safe:** never look at the Sun with or without optical aids.
